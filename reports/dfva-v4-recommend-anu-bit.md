## 1. DIAGNOSTIC SUMMARY — Basis: inferred

*This plan argues from the preceding scored evidence and market data; it is
interpretation, not observation.*

The Bachelor of Information Technology holds both gates — G1 on a staged, all-compulsory computing sequence (COMP1100, COMP1600, COMP2300, plus five 3000/4000-level computing courses) and G2 on assessed design trade-offs in COMP2300's simulator build and COMP1100's algorithm-selection assignment — but starts from a low position on both sub-scales: 4/15 Adaptive and 1/9 Workplace, against a field-of-education destination exposure of 83.3 that is itself an estimate.

Three items sit at the instrument floor — C5 (inquiry & evidence generation), W1 (professional communication & conduct) and W3 (work-situated learning) — each because the documented core carries no methods unit, no professional-genre or externally judged assessment, and no placement, practicum or live-client unit at all. The remaining five items (C1, C2, C3, C4, W2) each score 1/3: the relevant capability sits only in a program-level outcome statement, or as elective or tool-operation content, never as assessed core practice. The market report's signals bear most directly on C3, C4, W1 and W2 — the ACS Code of Professional Conduct, the absence of ICT statutory licensure, and the JSA and PwC findings on AI augmentation and exposure — while C1, C5 and W3 have to be argued from adjacent skill demand or a general augmentability finding, since no theme in the market report names them directly.

## 5. GATE GUARDRAILS — Basis: scored

| Gate | Status | Evidence carrying the gate | Constraint on redesign | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| G1 Disciplinary foundation | PASS | PASS anchor: a coherent specialist core with progressive technical depth. COMP1100 is documented as the first of three core computer science courses on programming, and the structure stages that sequence — programming and discrete mathematics in year 1 with COMP1600 Foundations of Computing, software construction and computer architecture in year 2, then five 3000/4000-level computing courses in year 3. A mathematics prerequisite gates entry. This is a disciplinary identity, not interchangeable generic content, so the FAIL condition does not hold. | Any lever above must preserve the compulsory programming, discrete-mathematics, foundations-of-computing, software-construction and computer-architecture sequence (COMP1100, COMP1600, COMP2300, and the five 3000/4000-level computing courses) and the mathematics prerequisite; folding an elective into the core (C5:1, C3:2, W3:1) must add to this specialist sequence, not displace it. | [[1]](https://www.teqsa.gov.au/guides-resources/resources/corporate-publications/assuring-quality-learning-gen-ai-integrated-future-role-adaptive-capabilities)[[6]](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjaa021) |
| G2 Decision-making under uncertainty | PASS | PASS anchor: assessed simulation work carrying design trade-off decisions. COMP2300's assessment is 40% practical assignments against a documented requirement to design and implement a processor in a digital simulator and to evaluate trade-offs between designs and account for the implications of machine-level choices; COMP1100's 30% assignments require determining which algorithmic approach fits a problem from a named set of alternatives. The FAIL condition — recall or scripted responses only — is contradicted by these design and selection tasks, even though half of the core marks sit in final exams. | Any lever above must preserve COMP2300's design-and-trade-off practical assignment and COMP1100's algorithmic-approach-selection assignment, since these carry the G2 pass; a lever that turns either into a fixed-recipe task — for example, over-specifying the "unfamiliar problem" required by C4:2 into a templated variant — would risk the gate regardless of its adaptiveness effect. | [16] |
