**C1 Distributed cognition & relational capability — 2/3**

> Effectively work in teams to solve complex, open-ended software engineering problems that require significant research and exploration;
>
> In this subject, students will actively engage in an interdisciplinary, collaborative and project-based learning environment, offering insights into the professional nature of engineering work.
>
> 2 small-team based projects requiring approximately 50-55 hours of work per student.
>
> A team-based project throughout the semester with code and reports, submitted in 3 parts.
>
> Individual moderation will be made on marks based on the contribution that individuals make to the team throughout the project.
>
> Students will work in teams to conceive, analyse, design, implement and test a non-trivial software system for an external client.
>
> Students will work in large teams to develop a non-trivial software system for an external client using agile software engineering methods.
>
> Participate collaboratively in a large software development team including communication, management, and quality assurance, utilising a variety of software engineering processes.

## 3. GATES — Basis: scored

- **G1 Disciplinary foundation:** PASS — The program documents a coherent, all-compulsory specialist software-engineering core staged across three years (Year 1/2/3 compulsory subjects) with progression noted between Year 2 and 3 core subjects, and is dual-accredited by Engineers Australia and the Australian Computer Society, satisfying the 'accredited sequence' route to PASS.
- **G2 Decision-making under uncertainty:** PASS — Core assessments require defended trade-off decisions and real-world capstone work with genuine uncertainty and accountability: SWEN90007's ILO requires students to 'Make suitable trade-offs between different architectures', ENGR90051 requires students to 'Justify design decisions throughout the design process', and SWEN90017's capstone explicitly frames real-world software delivery 'on time and within budget' for an external client as 'a challenging task', with students required to 'Interpret the needs of external stakeholders'.

## 6. EVIDENCE CONFIDENCE & LIMITATIONS — Basis: reported

**Panel A metadata.** Basis tier **field** (field grain), n = 0, 14 destination titles, 3 at medium mapping confidence. Index variant AIOE-2021; destination titles mapped to the published Felten index. Basis note: field-of-education occupation list (ATO-linked), not this program's own graduates

**Panel C provenance.** Scored from the 2026 handbook capture (35 pages) and verified on 2026-08-30: adversarial refutation of every level-3 score and every gate PASS (run), verbatim evidence check (run), and the construct-boundary pass.

**Ambiguities and construct-boundary notes** (4):

- C2: ENGR90051's assessed 'Peer feedback + evaluation' (15%) component could be read as criterion-referenced appraisal of quality (level 2), but the extract does not state explicit quality criteria/standards for it, so scored at level 1 per the direction rule.
- C4: SWEN90017/SWEN90018's capstone sequence could be read as the 'self-scoped capstone' of level 3, but the problem is supplied by an external client rather than self-scoped by students, so scored at level 2.
- W1: the recurring external-client work in SWEN90009/SWEN90014/SWEN90017 could satisfy level 3's requirement that work be judged by a real external audience/practitioner with professional conduct as an explicit criterion, but the extract attributes grading to supervisors/staff and never names professional conduct as an assessed criterion, so scored at level 2.
- W3: the compulsory Engineering Practice Hurdle could constitute workplace-situated learning for students who choose the internship route, but STEP's content is undocumented and the hurdle can be satisfied without any placement, so scored at level 1 rather than level 2.

**Not scoreable from the handbook** (1):

- SWEN90018 (Masters Advanced Software Project Part 2), a Year 3 compulsory subject, has no overview/assessment page captured in this extract — only its title appears in the course-structure subject list — so its contribution to C1/C4/W2/W3 beyond what SWEN90017 (Part 1) documents could not be verified.

**Standing v4 limitations.**

- Scores describe **documented curriculum intent**, not demonstrated graduate
  capability; the extrapolation warrant is constructive alignment, stated as an
  assumption (Kane [17]).
- Indigenous data governance is not scored as a distinct construct; C3 level 3
  counts it where taught (CARE Principles; Lodge et al. 2025 Principle 4 [[2]](https://www.acses.edu.au/publication/australian-framework-for-artificial-intelligence-in-higher-education/)).
- Perception/manipulation (Frey & Osborne's third bottleneck [[7]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.techfore.2016.08.019)) is unscored.
- A high W3 score describes curriculum design, not student welfare: required
  placements can disadvantage students who cannot afford unpaid work or
  relocation (Accord [[30]](https://www.education.gov.au/australian-universities-accord/resources/final-report); Fawns et al. [[24]](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2024.2404634) on ableism and elitism).
- W2 is scored against each discipline's own criterion situation [[22]](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02504676), so
  cross-disciplinary W2 comparison is weaker than within-discipline comparison.
- W3 excludes simulation by design (simulation scores in W2), so programs whose
  professional norm is high-fidelity simulation may score low on W3 for a
  defensible pedagogic choice. Read the two sub-scores together.
- The authentic-assessment employability evidence rests largely on student
  self-report [[25]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.stueduc.2021.101030); W2's warrant is documented design fidelity plus the
  assessment-drives-learning mechanism [26], not demonstrated employment effects.
- Work-integrated learning is NOT claimed to raise employment rates — Jackson &
  Collings [[28]](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10734-017-0216-z) found it does not. W3's warrant is skill development, employment
  relevance and professional identity formation [[29]](https://doi.org/10.1080/07294360.2016.1139551).
