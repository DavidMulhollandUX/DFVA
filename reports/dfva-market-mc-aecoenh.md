### Theme 5 — australian economists are not governed by a binding AI-specific licence or professional code; the applicabl…

Australian economists are not governed by a binding AI-specific licence or professional code; the applicable national standard for their use of AI in analysis is the voluntary eight-principle AI Ethics Framework released by the Department of Industry in November 2019, which is not legally enforceable. The peak body, the Economic Society of Australia, is a learned society and has issued no binding AI conduct code, so AI use by economists remains at the voluntary-standards tier. (Economists; lane L1, regulatory and statutory instruments.)

Sources: [Australian Government, Department of Industry, Innovation and Science (with CSIRO Data61), Australia's AI Ethics Principles](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/australias-ai-ethics-principles) (2019-11-07).

Scope: Australian economists and other professionals using AI in economic analysis; no occupation-specific binding code applies (contrast with accountants under the TPB or engineers under Engineers Australia, which have registration frameworks).

**Bearing:** not stated in the profession record.

## 5. CURRICULUM IMPLICATIONS

| # | Implication | Dimension | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| CI-1 | The five falling clerical skills (§4) sit inside a family Theme 2 (§3) names as "the clear automation-exposed group"; a program whose only link to entry-level office work is administrative or data-handling tasks graduates students into a shrinking segment of that family. | C2, C3 | Add a unit or module requiring students to evaluate and correct AI-generated outputs against a defined standard, rather than only produce first-draft work (cost: new assessment design and marking rubric). |
| CI-2 | Theme 3 (§3) obliges auditors to verify automated anomaly-detection algorithms and keep documented AI audit trails; Audit sampling stays in demand (§4) but as a verification skill layered on automated tooling, not a manual-only technique. | C3, C5 | Extend an existing audit or assurance unit to include an assessed task verifying the output of an automated detection tool against a sampling standard (cost: access to or simulation of an anomaly-detection tool, plus marking time). |
| CI-3 | Theme 4 (§3) reports an acute shortage of accounting graduates skilled in multi-capital greenhouse-gas assurance and climate-risk governance under the mandatory Australian Sustainability Reporting Standards; this is a named gap the program can address directly. | C4, W2 | Introduce an elective or embedded module on sustainability assurance and climate-risk governance (cost: new curriculum content and, if elective, competition for enrolment against existing options). |
| CI-4 | Theme 6 (§3) finds AI now embedded across five economics research streams, including forecasting and prediction; a program that teaches forecasting only through traditional methods does not evidence the human-plus-tool coordination increasingly expected of economists' analytical work. | C1, C5 | Require at least one forecasting or modelling assessment that documents the division of work between the student and an AI or statistical tool (cost: revised assessment brief and rubric, no new unit needed). |
| CI-5 | Theme 5 (§3) records no binding AI-specific code for economists; the Economic Society of Australia has issued none, so graduates cannot rely on a professional body to define responsible AI use in their own analytical work. | C3 | Add an explicit AI-governance component to an existing methods unit, drawing on the voluntary national AI Ethics Principles rather than an occupation-specific code (cost: staff time to develop teaching material, no new unit). |
