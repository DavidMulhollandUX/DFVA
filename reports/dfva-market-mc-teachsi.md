### Theme 1 — in Australia, entry to the teaching profession is regulated nationally through AITSL's Accreditation of ini…

In Australia, entry to the teaching profession is regulated nationally through AITSL's Accreditation of initial teacher education programs in Australia: Standards and Procedures (2025), under which every initial teacher education program must be accredited so its graduates meet the Australian Professional Standards for Teachers at the Graduate career stage. State and territory teacher registration authorities (for example VIT in Victoria and NESA in NSW) then register teachers to practise under their respective Education Acts, so Secondary School Teachers, Except Special and Vocational Education requires an accredited teaching qualification and registration to lawfully teach in an Australian school. (Secondary School Teachers, Except Special and Vocational Education; lane L1, regulatory and statutory instruments.)

Sources: [Australian Institute for Teaching and School Leadership (AITSL), Accreditation of initial teacher education programs in Australia: Standards](https://aitsl.edu.au/tools-resources/resource/accreditation-of-initial-teacher-education-programs-in-australia---standards-and-procedures) (2025).

Scope: Australian school teachers (all states/territories); binds who may lawfully teach. Does not cover unregistered paraprofessionals or out-of-school tutoring.

**Bearing:** not stated in the profession record.

### Theme 2 — jobs and Skills Australia's Gen AI Capacity Study (August 2025) scores 358 ANZSCO occupations on augmentati…

Jobs and Skills Australia's Gen AI Capacity Study (August 2025) scores 358 ANZSCO occupations on augmentation and automation exposure using a task-level ILO/Felten framework. It finds most Australian workers face low automation risk, but education-professional occupations cluster toward the higher-exposure end because their core tasks (lesson planning, resource creation, feedback, assessment drafting) are language- and knowledge-intensive and readily supported by generative AI. (Secondary School Teachers, Except Special and Vocational Education; lane L2, scholarly and grey-literature studies.)

Sources: [Jobs and Skills Australia, Our Gen AI Transition: Exposures, Adaptation, Dynamism (Generative AI Capacity Study)](https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/studies/generative-artificial-intelligence-capacity-study/occupation-data-on-ai-exposure) (2025-08).

Scope: Australian occupations nationally; model-derived task-level estimates, not employment forecasts.

**Bearing:** not stated in the profession record.

### Theme 3 — in August 2026, state education departments moved to curb student AI cheating in ways that reshape teacher…

In August 2026, state education departments moved to curb student AI cheating in ways that reshape teacher assessment design: NSW proposed banning unsupervised take-home assignments for Years 11-12 (prompted by more than 1,000 recorded cheating cases in 2025) and NESA's 2024 AI-in-schools policy treats unapproved AI use as an academic-integrity breach, while Victoria will impose primary-school screen-time caps from Term 1 2027. Teachers must therefore collect evidence of learning through oral, performance and in-person modes less exposed to AI misuse. (Elementary School Teachers, Except Special Education; lane L3, trade and professional press.)

Sources: [ABC News, How education departments across the country are cracking down on students using AI to cheat](https://abc.net.au/news/2026-08-25/how-education-departments-across-country-are-cracking-down-on-ai/107026976) (2026-08-25).

Scope: Australian state school systems; reporting, not a primary study. NSW/VIC specific examples.

**Bearing:** not stated in the profession record.

### Theme 4 — responsible AI Australia's 2026 education brief notes the national gen-AI schools framework is implemented…

Responsible AI Australia's 2026 education brief notes the national gen-AI schools framework is implemented by states through binding policies, and that schools must run privacy due diligence before procuring AI tools (data storage, model training on inputs, third-party sharing), prohibit entering student or staff personal information into public gen-AI tools, and keep humans in the loop on judgements such as student-achievement marking and report writing. These constraints define the boundaries of AI adoption a teacher can undertake. (Elementary School Teachers, Except Special Education; lane L3, trade and professional press.)

Sources: [Responsible AI Australia, AI Regulation in Australian Education (2026): Schools, Universities, Rules and Updates](https://responsibleaiaustralia.com.au/industries/education) (2026).

Scope: Australian school education; practitioner/policy brief, not a primary study.

**Bearing:** not stated in the profession record.

### Theme 5 — the Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools (endorsed by Education Ministers; June 2025 review) s…

The Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools (endorsed by Education Ministers; June 2025 review) sets six principles - teaching and learning, human and social wellbeing, transparency, fairness, accountability, and privacy/security/safety - to guide responsible gen-AI use by teachers, students and school leaders. It is national policy implemented through state and system binding rules rather than legislation, and directly shapes what teachers are permitted and expected to do with AI in classrooms. (Middle School Teachers, Except Special and Vocational Education; lane L3, trade and professional press.)

Sources: [Australian Government Department of Education, Australian Framework for Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) in Schools](https://education.gov.au/schooling/resources/australian-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence-ai-schools) (2025-06).

Scope: Australian school education nationally; policy framework, not a primary study.

**Bearing:** not stated in the profession record.

### Theme 6 — office clerks handle personal and commercial information; the Privacy Act 1988 APPs bind their APP-entity e…

Office clerks handle personal and commercial information; the Privacy Act 1988 APPs bind their APP-entity employers and the Fair Work Act 2009 requires seven-year employee-record retention. No occupation-specific licensing regime governs general office clerks. (Office Clerks, General; lane L1, regulatory and statutory instruments.)

Sources: [Jacmac Law, Accessing employment records: Privacy Act and Fair Work Act intersection](https://jacmac.com.au/insights/accessing-employment-records) (2025).

Scope: Applies via employer; employee-records exemption; no specific regulator for the occupation.

**Bearing:** not stated in the profession record.

## 5. CURRICULUM IMPLICATIONS

| # | Implication | Dimension | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| CI-1 | Theme 2 (§3) finds education-professional occupations cluster toward the higher-exposure end of the Jobs and Skills Australia study because core teaching tasks — lesson planning, resource creation, feedback, assessment drafting — are language-intensive and readily supported by generative AI. For an internship placing graduates directly into these tasks, the open question is whether trainees learn to evaluate AI-drafted material rather than only produce it. | C3 (digital and AI literacy, including governance); C2 (hybrid metacognition and evaluative judgement) | Add a supervised placement task requiring trainees to critique and revise an AI-drafted lesson plan or assessment item before use (cost: supervisor time to design and mark the task), or leave current placement tasks unchanged (cost: the exposure raised in Theme 2 goes unaddressed in the internship). |
| CI-2 | Theme 3 (§3) records state departments moving assessment away from unsupervised take-home tasks toward oral, performance and in-person modes to limit AI misuse (NSW proposal for Years 11-12; NESA's 2024 academic-integrity policy). A secondary-teaching internship's own placement assessment is a model for the modes trainees will later design themselves. | W2 (authentic task design) | Audit placement assessment tasks against the oral, performance and in-person mix cited in Theme 3, and adjust the balance where it is skewed toward take-home work (cost: curriculum review time), or leave the current mix unreviewed (cost: no evidence the placement models the shift Theme 3 describes). |
| CI-3 | Theme 4 and Theme 5 (§3) set obligations a practising teacher must meet when using generative AI: privacy due diligence before adopting a tool, no student or staff personal information entered into public tools, and a human kept in the loop on judgements such as marking and report writing. Whether the internship's professional-conduct component names these obligations, rather than treating AI use as a generic topic, is falsifiable against its current content. | C3 (digital and AI literacy, including governance); W1 (professional communication and conduct) | Map one placement seminar or module explicitly to the Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools' six principles and the privacy-due-diligence obligation in Theme 4 (cost: one seminar's preparation and delivery time), or continue with generic professional-conduct content that does not name the framework (cost: trainees may reach placement without framework-specific grounding). |
| CI-4 | Theme 1 (§3) sets accreditation under AITSL's Standards and Procedures (2025) and state registration (for example VIT, NESA) as the lawful entry gate to secondary teaching, tying the internship's placement structure and hours to a specific accreditation edition rather than a general professional-experience expectation. | W3 (work-situated learning) | Confirm the internship's placement hours and supervision structure are checked against the 2025 AITSL Standards and Procedures edition (cost: an administrative mapping exercise against the current standard), or treat the existing structure as current without a check (cost: a standards revision could pass unnoticed). |
