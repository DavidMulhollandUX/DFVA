## 3. GATES — Basis: scored

- **G1 Disciplinary foundation:** PASS — PASS on the staged prerequisite/progression condition: the program documents a compulsory six-unit disciplinary core plus a required eight-unit major carrying a documented level-1 to level-3 progression, with an overall rule requiring at least four level-3 units from the Faculty of Business and Economics. That is a specialist core with progressive depth in one discipline, not the generic or interchangeable content the FAIL condition describes.
- **G2 Decision-making under uncertainty:** PASS — PASS: assessment is not confined to recall or scripted responses. Course outcome 6 requires critical evaluation of the implications of various options, MGC1010 requires critical evaluation of managerial decisions against ethical, environmental and social responsibility and carries Project assessment at 40%, and ETC1000 requires modelling uncertainty and assessing risk. A capstone unit consolidating the course is required of every student. The evidence is thin on documented defence of trade-offs, since assessment tasks are named only by type and weight, but the FAIL condition (recall or scripted responses only) is clearly not met.

## 6. EVIDENCE CONFIDENCE & LIMITATIONS — Basis: reported

**Panel A metadata.** Basis tier **field** (field grain), n = 0, 13 destination titles, 1 at medium mapping confidence. Index variant AIOE-2021; destination titles mapped to the published Felten index. Basis note: field-of-education occupation list (ATO-linked), not this program's own graduates

**Panel C provenance.** Scored from the 2026 handbook capture (9 pages) and verified on 2026-09-07: adversarial refutation of every level-3 score and every gate PASS (run), verbatim evidence check (run), and the construct-boundary pass.

**Ambiguities and construct-boundary notes** (7):

- C1 straddled 1 and 2: MGC1010 is documented as taught in teams and carries a Project (40%) and Demonstration (30%), which could be group-assessed, but the extract never states that any assessment task is collaborative. Resolved DOWN to 1 by the lower-level rule and R2 (an outcome that is never documented as assessed scores 1).
- C2 straddled 1 and 2: BEX3500's Portfolio (40%) with its reflective outcome would satisfy the level-2 'portfolio with standards' route, and BEX3350 assesses evaluation of individual and peer contributions. Resolved DOWN to 1 because both units are single options within a capstone portfolio of roughly thirty units, so neither is core assessment for the cohort.
- C3 straddled 0 and 1: no AI content of any kind appears in the extract, which points at 0, but level 0's declarative negative is falsified by documented core content (privacy and data protection law in BTC1110, data analysis in ETC1000) and by the digital-tools outcome in BEX3350. Scored 1 as the best-fitting anchor, not as an upward resolution of a genuine tie; nothing in the extract supports level 2.
- C4 straddled 1 and 2: MGC1010 and ETC1000 apply methods to case-based and real-world scenarios and both carry Project assessment, which could constitute application to novel problems. Resolved DOWN to 1 because the assessment tables give only task type and weight, so no assessment is documented as requiring an unfamiliar case or dataset.
- W1 straddled 1 and 2, and W2 straddled 1 and 2, on the same structural fact: Part C is required of every student, but the required choice is one unit from a portfolio spanning taught capstones, consulting projects, international experiences and internships. Only two of those units (BEX3500, BEX3350) are in the extract, so no professional-genre or end-to-end professional task can be attributed to every student. Both resolved DOWN.
- W3 straddled 1 and 2 for the same reason: industry placements and a tax clinic sit inside the required Part C choice, so a student may satisfy Part C without any workplace placement. Resolved DOWN to 1.
- One construct, one home: the consulting-project evidence in BEX3350 could read into C1 (client/stakeholder work), W1 (professional communication), W2 (professional task) and W3 (live client project). It is quoted as context for why each item did not reach level 2, and scores in none of them, because the unit is not required.

**Standing v4 limitations.**

- Scores describe **documented curriculum intent**, not demonstrated graduate
  capability; the extrapolation warrant is constructive alignment, stated as an
  assumption (Kane [17]).
- Indigenous data governance is not scored as a distinct construct; C3 level 3
  counts it where taught (CARE Principles; Lodge et al. 2025 Principle 4 [[2]](https://www.acses.edu.au/publication/australian-framework-for-artificial-intelligence-in-higher-education/)).
- Perception/manipulation (Frey & Osborne's third bottleneck [[7]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.techfore.2016.08.019)) is unscored.
- A high W3 score describes curriculum design, not student welfare: required
  placements can disadvantage students who cannot afford unpaid work or
  relocation (Accord [[30]](https://www.education.gov.au/australian-universities-accord/resources/final-report); Fawns et al. [[24]](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2024.2404634) on ableism and elitism).
- W2 is scored against each discipline's own criterion situation [[22]](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02504676), so
  cross-disciplinary W2 comparison is weaker than within-discipline comparison.
- W3 excludes simulation by design (simulation scores in W2), so programs whose
  professional norm is high-fidelity simulation may score low on W3 for a
  defensible pedagogic choice. Read the two sub-scores together.
- The authentic-assessment employability evidence rests largely on student
  self-report [[25]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.stueduc.2021.101030); W2's warrant is documented design fidelity plus the
  assessment-drives-learning mechanism [26], not demonstrated employment effects.
- Work-integrated learning is NOT claimed to raise employment rates — Jackson &
  Collings [[28]](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10734-017-0216-z) found it does not. W3's warrant is skill development, employment
  relevance and professional identity formation [[29]](https://doi.org/10.1080/07294360.2016.1139551).
