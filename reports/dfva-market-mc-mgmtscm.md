## 3. CURRENT DISCUSSION SIGNALS — MEDIUM CONFIDENCE

**What these sources are.** An institutional/scholarly AI-exposure study (Carnegie Mellon-affiliated, published via the Washington Center for Equitable Growth), an Australian government occupational exposure study reported through a third-party aggregator (Jobs and Skills Australia's Gen AI Capacity Study), trade press covering an analyst-firm survey (Material Handling & Logistics covering Gartner) and a recruiting-research firm's own synthesis (SCM Talent Group), a trade-magazine feature compiling named industry experts' predictions (Inbound Logistics), and two professional-body/trade-press pieces quoting named practitioners directly (CIPS; Supply Chain Dive). Direct extraction from X or LinkedIn was **not** performed and no professional forum was sampled — "discourse" here means these named institutional, trade-press and quoted-practitioner sources, not a survey of practitioner sentiment. Every claim below is carried forward from the occupation profile record (0803), where it is already marked sourced, scoped or corrected; none required removal for this report.

### Theme 1 — the occupation carries substantial measured AI task exposure by two independent measures, though neither is a direct, current-Australian, program-specific figure

A 2025 Carnegie Mellon-affiliated study using a frequency-weighted extension of the Eloundou et al. AI-exposure methodology found that among more than 200,000 US logistics managers (operations, warehouse and transportation managers), more than **90%** of tasks are susceptible to AI-driven automation, with nearly all of those classified as core rather than peripheral activities ([Washington Center for Equitable Growth, 10 July 2025](https://equitablegrowth.org/adoption-of-generative-ai-will-have-different-effects-across-jobs-in-the-u-s-logistics-workforce/)). Separately, Jobs and Skills Australia's Gen AI Capacity Study assigns Supply, Distribution and Procurement Managers — the ANZSCO grouping supply chain managers sit within — a Gen AI exposure score of **5.9/10**, with a 53% automation component and a 74% augmentation component, against a 56,700-worker occupation base ([Will AI Take My Job (Australia)](https://www.willaitakemyjob.com.au/occupation/supply-distribution-and-procurement-managers), reporting the JSA study).

The CMU-affiliated figure is US-only and restricted to the Transportation and Warehousing industry (NAICS 48-49); it measures task susceptibility to LLM-driven acceleration, not realised job loss or headcount reduction. The JSA figure is Australian and occupation-specific, but is a third-party aggregator's presentation of numbers attributed to a government study — the primary Jobs and Skills Australia page or PDF was not independently confirmed — and the occupation grouping spans Distribution and Procurement Managers too, not supply chain managers alone.

**Bearing:** C3. AI/digital-tools content in this program currently sits only among elective options (MGMT90267 Artificial Intelligence in Organisations); the one Discipline Core unit that names AI (MGMT90297 Supply Chain Technology & Info Mgmt) states an outcome on evaluating "the costs, benefits and risks... of introducing new technologies," but its listed assessment items do not name AI-specific content in the verified evidence extract, holding the item at level 1 against sourced task-exposure figures running from roughly half to over 90% of tasks.

### Theme 2 — a global leadership survey signals expectations of reduced entry-level hiring, while a job-postings analysis shows AI-related roles skewing toward experienced hires

A Gartner survey of 509 global supply chain leaders, fielded July-October 2025, found 55% expect agentic AI to reduce entry-level hiring needs and 51% expect it to drive overall workforce reductions, while 86% agreed AI adoption requires new talent-pipeline processes ([Material Handling & Logistics, 27 February 2026](https://www.mhlnews.com/labor-management/news/55360308/agentic-ai-to-replace-entry-level-supply-chain-hiring)). Consistent with that direction, SCM Talent Group — a supply-chain-focused recruiting and research firm — reported on 5 February 2026, citing Indeed job-posting data, that **85%** of AI-related supply chain job openings in 2025 targeted mid-to-senior level roles rather than entry-level positions ([SCM Talent Group](https://scmtalent.com/supply-chain-ai-jobs)).

Both figures reflect stated expectations or a recruiting firm's own synthesis of job-posting data, not observed hiring outcomes verified against a primary dataset; the Gartner sample is global rather than Australian, and the SCM Talent Group figure describes the US market. Neither establishes an Australian entry-level hiring rate for this program's graduates.

**Bearing:** W3. The compulsory Capstone structure lists a work-integrated-learning practicum (BUSA90473/BUSA90485) as one of several substitutable options alongside two subjects marked "Not available in 2026" and an entirely separate, non-placement Research route, matching level 1's "optional internship" anchor rather than a required core placement — meaning graduates enter a labour market where surveyed leaders expect reduced entry-level hiring without a guaranteed applied placement to differentiate them.

### Theme 3 — named practitioners and trade press frame the near-term trajectory as evolving roles and cautious, incremental adoption, but the small number of quoted voices diverge sharply and only one is confirmed Australian

Trade publication Inbound Logistics reported in November 2025 that industry experts expect AI to evolve supply chain and logistics manager roles toward AI-oversight positions such as "warehouse AI specialists," with new hybrid titles emerging — for example predictive logistics manager, AI forecast coach — while humans remain in the loop for final decisions ([Inbound Logistics, November 2025](https://www.inboundlogistics.com/articles/what-new-job-will-ai-create-in-logistics-supply-chain-management/)). Named procurement and supply chain practitioners quoted by CIPS diverge sharply on their own function's trajectory: one predicts a "lights out" supply chain function by 2028, another frames the change as different jobs demanding different skills ([CIPS, 13 July 2026](https://www.cips.org/knowledge-and-insight/articles/the-future-of-ai-in-procurement-and-supply)). Separately, senior procurement and supply chain executives speaking to Supply Chain Dive described a cautious, incremental adoption posture — "we don't have a blank check"; "the juice has to be worth the squeeze" — aiming AI mainly at removing "grunt work" so staff can focus on higher-value tasks ([Supply Chain Dive, 21 May 2026](https://www.supplychaindive.com/news/procurement-leaders-urge-incremental-ai-adoption-to-avoid-heavy-spend/820557/)).

The Inbound Logistics piece compiles qualitative predictions from named experts, not a survey or dataset. Of the two CIPS-quoted practitioners, only one (Sarah Simpson, Bega Group) is at an Australian organisation; the other, Belinda West, is Head of Non-Trade Procurement at Woolworths Holdings, the South African retailer unrelated to the Australian Woolworths Group despite the shared name — this is not two Australian practitioner perspectives. The Supply Chain Dive quotes come from named executives at large US enterprises speaking at an industry event, skewing toward large-enterprise procurement leadership rather than mid-level supply chain or logistics managers, and do not establish how common this posture is in Australia.

**Bearing:** C2 and W1. Both items rest on the same optional Capstone-route evidence (BUSA90485's peer-review-then-revise cycle and host-company presentation), which the program's own evidence file already discounts for W3 on identical grounds — meaning no genuinely compulsory unit gives students assessed practice in critiquing AI or peer output, or in communicating to a real external audience, at precisely the moment named practitioners disagree sharply about the profession's own trajectory and executives describe judgement-heavy, case-by-case adoption decisions.
