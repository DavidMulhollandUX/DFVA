### Theme 1 — the Australian Institute of Landscape Architects (AILA) operates a national professional-recognition scheme…

The Australian Institute of Landscape Architects (AILA) operates a national professional-recognition scheme under which members use the title 'Registered Landscape Architect'. Unlike architecture, landscape architecture is not a protected/statutorily registered title under state Architects Acts, so there is no government register compelling registration to practise; recognition is a private professional-body credential, and some states (e.g. Queensland) do not require it for contracts. (Landscape Architects; lane L1, regulatory and statutory instruments.)

Sources: [Australian Institute of Landscape Architects (via profession summary), Australian Institute of Landscape Architects — professional recogniti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Institute_of_Landscape_Architects).

Scope: Private professional-body recognition, not a government licence. Does not bind practice by statute; some agencies require AILA recognition as a contract prerequisite.

**Bearing:** not stated in the profession record.

## 5. CURRICULUM IMPLICATIONS

| # | Implication | Dimension | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| CI-1 | The Australian Institute of Landscape Architects' recognition scheme is a private credential that some states, including Queensland, do not require for contracts (Theme 1, §3); the program cannot rely on that credential alone to certify practice-readiness across every jurisdiction. | W1 | Add an internally assessed practice-readiness milestone that stands apart from Australian Institute of Landscape Architects membership (cost: extra assessment design and marking load), or keep the existing recognition pathway as an elective step for students (cost: none, but the Queensland gap stays open). |
| CI-2 | Theme 2 (§3) finds augmentation, not automation, dominant for the professional roles this program's graduates enter, and §4 carries design, urban, client, construction and environmental as the recurring skills that finding covers; the signal is that graduates need to direct AI-assisted design tools, not that the underlying design skill is falling away. | C3 | Add an assessed exercise on directing AI-assisted design and documentation tools inside an existing compulsory design studio (cost: studio redesign and staff training), or offer the same exercise as an elective (cost: lower, reaches fewer students). |
| CI-3 | The Planning Institute of Australia's PlanTech guidelines ask planners to keep a person in the loop on AI-assisted decisions (Theme 3, §3), and Signal 2 (§2) records 69 current postings for Urban and Regional Planners, the job family this degree's urban design component feeds; graduates need to demonstrate that oversight, not only operate planning software. | C2 | Add a criterion-referenced critique task on AI-assisted planning output to an existing studio or seminar (cost: rubric design and marking load), or leave the critique inside current studio feedback without a separate assessed task (cost: none, but the oversight capability stays unverified). |
| CI-4 | Signal 1 and Signal 2 (§2) name employers spanning private design and planning consultancies (for example Urbis, HASSELL, AECOM, Arup) alongside local government bodies (for example City of Rockingham, Bayside Council, Townsville City Council); graduates need to work with both private-practice and public-sector or regulatory clients, not only a studio design brief. | W2 | Structure a compulsory studio or the capstone around a public-sector or regulatory client brief alongside the existing private-practice brief (cost: partnership sourcing and assessment redesign), or keep the current studio brief mix unchanged (cost: none, but the public-sector coordination capability stays unverified). |
