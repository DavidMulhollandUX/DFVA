### Theme 1 — in Australia, entry to the teaching profession is regulated nationally through AITSL's Accreditation of ini…

In Australia, entry to the teaching profession is regulated nationally through AITSL's Accreditation of initial teacher education programs in Australia: Standards and Procedures (2025), under which every initial teacher education program must be accredited so its graduates meet the Australian Professional Standards for Teachers at the Graduate career stage. State and territory teacher registration authorities (for example VIT in Victoria and NESA in NSW) then register teachers to practise under their respective Education Acts, so Elementary School Teachers, Except Special Education requires an accredited teaching qualification and registration to lawfully teach in an Australian school. (Elementary School Teachers, Except Special Education; lane L1, regulatory and statutory instruments.)

Sources: [Australian Institute for Teaching and School Leadership (AITSL), Accreditation of initial teacher education programs in Australia: Standards](https://aitsl.edu.au/tools-resources/resource/accreditation-of-initial-teacher-education-programs-in-australia---standards-and-procedures) (2025).

Scope: Australian school teachers (all states/territories); binds who may lawfully teach. Does not cover unregistered paraprofessionals or out-of-school tutoring.

**Bearing:** not stated in the profession record.

### Theme 2 — jobs and Skills Australia's Gen AI Capacity Study (August 2025) scores 358 ANZSCO occupations on augmentati…

Jobs and Skills Australia's Gen AI Capacity Study (August 2025) scores 358 ANZSCO occupations on augmentation and automation exposure using a task-level ILO/Felten framework. It finds most Australian workers face low automation risk, but education-professional occupations cluster toward the higher-exposure end because their core tasks (lesson planning, resource creation, feedback, assessment drafting) are language- and knowledge-intensive and readily supported by generative AI. (Elementary School Teachers, Except Special Education; lane L2, scholarly and grey-literature studies.)

Sources: [Jobs and Skills Australia, Our Gen AI Transition: Exposures, Adaptation, Dynamism (Generative AI Capacity Study)](https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/studies/generative-artificial-intelligence-capacity-study/occupation-data-on-ai-exposure) (2025-08).

Scope: Australian occupations nationally; model-derived task-level estimates, not employment forecasts.

**Bearing:** not stated in the profession record.

### Theme 3 — in August 2026, state education departments moved to curb student AI cheating in ways that reshape teacher…

In August 2026, state education departments moved to curb student AI cheating in ways that reshape teacher assessment design: NSW proposed banning unsupervised take-home assignments for Years 11-12 (prompted by more than 1,000 recorded cheating cases in 2025) and NESA's 2024 AI-in-schools policy treats unapproved AI use as an academic-integrity breach, while Victoria will impose primary-school screen-time caps from Term 1 2027. Teachers must therefore collect evidence of learning through oral, performance and in-person modes less exposed to AI misuse. (Secondary School Teachers, Except Special and Vocational Education; lane L3, trade and professional press.)

Sources: [ABC News, How education departments across the country are cracking down on students using AI to cheat](https://abc.net.au/news/2026-08-25/how-education-departments-across-country-are-cracking-down-on-ai/107026976) (2026-08-25).

Scope: Australian state school systems; reporting, not a primary study. NSW/VIC specific examples.

**Bearing:** not stated in the profession record.

### Theme 4 — responsible AI Australia's 2026 education brief notes the national gen-AI schools framework is implemented…

Responsible AI Australia's 2026 education brief notes the national gen-AI schools framework is implemented by states through binding policies, and that schools must run privacy due diligence before procuring AI tools (data storage, model training on inputs, third-party sharing), prohibit entering student or staff personal information into public gen-AI tools, and keep humans in the loop on judgements such as student-achievement marking and report writing. These constraints define the boundaries of AI adoption a teacher can undertake. (Secondary School Teachers, Except Special and Vocational Education; lane L3, trade and professional press.)

Sources: [Responsible AI Australia, AI Regulation in Australian Education (2026): Schools, Universities, Rules and Updates](https://responsibleaiaustralia.com.au/industries/education) (2026).

Scope: Australian school education; practitioner/policy brief, not a primary study.

**Bearing:** not stated in the profession record.

The Medical Board of Australia and AHPRA mandate that clinical decision-making, procedural execution, and prescription authorization remain strict, non-delegable human medical responsibilities that cannot be deferred to diagnostic algorithms. (Physicians, Surgeons, and Medical Specialists; lane L1, regulatory and statutory instruments.)

Sources: [Medical Board of Australia / AHPRA, Good Medical Practice: A Code of Conduct for Doctors in Australia](https://www.medicalboard.gov.au/Codes-Guidelines-Policies/Code-of-conduct.aspx) (2024-09-01).

Scope: All registered medical practitioners across public and private healthcare sectors.

**Bearing:** C3 and W1 and G1.

Physical bedside examinations, intraoperative procedural adjustments, and acute clinical crisis management demonstrate near-zero automated substitution potential, anchoring long-term workforce durability. (Physicians, Surgeons, and Medical Specialists; lane L2, scholarly and grey-literature studies.)

Sources: [Medical Journal of Australia (MJA), Artificial Intelligence in Australian Clinical Medicine: Boundaries of Diagnostic Support vs Clinical Ac](https://doi.org/10.5694/mja2.52301) (2025-06-15).

## 5. CURRICULUM IMPLICATIONS

| # | Implication | Dimension | Action |

| CI-1 | Regulators for both destination clusters treat professional judgement as non-delegable: AITSL's accreditation standards for teaching (Theme 1, §3) and the medical regulator's conduct code for clinical practice (Theme 5, §3) both keep human decision-making at the centre of the role, so the curriculum needs to build and assess that judgement directly rather than assume it follows from content knowledge. | C2, W1 | Add or strengthen a core assessment task that requires graduates to justify a professional judgement against a named standard, with feedback on the reasoning, not only the outcome (cost: assessment redesign and marking load). |
| CI-2 | Job advertisements for the medical destination cluster (Signal 3, §2) most often name clinical diagnostic acumen and bedside procedural skill, and Theme 6 (§3) finds these tasks near-zero automatable, so a program preparing clinical educators should assess how well graduates can teach these skills in an authentic, practice-based setting, not only transmit them. | W2, W3 | Expand work-situated or simulated clinical-teaching placements with a structured assessment of an authentic bedside or procedural teaching task (cost: placement or simulation-supervision capacity). |
| CI-3 | State moves to curb AI-assisted assessment cheating are pushing school assessment toward oral, performance and in-person modes (Theme 3, §3), and a separate brief requires a human in the loop on judgements such as marking (Theme 4, §3); a program that prepares people to design assessment for their own learners should model that shift in its own curriculum. | C3, W2 | Build one assessment task around designing an oral, performance or in-person assessment resistant to AI misuse for the learner's own teaching or clinical context (cost: curriculum design time; no added marking load if it replaces an existing task). |
