## 3. CURRENT DISCUSSION SIGNALS — MEDIUM CONFIDENCE

**What these sources are.** Trade press (Publishers Weekly, The Art Newspaper, American Alliance of Museums' Museum magazine), a peer-reviewed scoping review (MDPI), a scholarly preprint (Stanford Digital Economy Lab on arXiv), and two regulatory/professional-body instruments (the Fair Work Commission's Book Industry Award and the AICCM Code of Ethics and Code of Practice). Direct extraction from X or LinkedIn was **not** performed as a targeted sample, and no professional forum was systematically sampled — the underlying profession records do carry a generic 30-day harvest of X, Reddit, GitHub, Hacker News and YouTube items, but that harvest is self-selected, largely off-topic to publishing and editorial work, and is not used as theme evidence here for that reason.

### Theme 1 — publishers already report meaningful AI use, and peer publishing-education programs are responding with dedicated AI-literacy coursework

Publishers Weekly reported on 2025-11-28 that 63% of surveyed respondents said their publishing companies use AI in some manner, and that publishing-education programs — the NYU Center for Publishing and Portland State University are named — have added AI-literacy coursework for copy editors and developmental editors, while freelance editors described particular pressure to use AI tools to stay competitive ([The Publishing Workshops Taking a Red Pen to AI](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/industry-news/publisher-news/article/99192-the-publishing-workshops-taking-a-red-pen-to-ai.html), Publishers Weekly, 2025-11-28).

This supports a claim about one trade publication's report of industry self-described AI use and about two named US institutions' curriculum response; it does not establish an industry-wide adoption rate, since the underlying company survey's methodology and sample size are not disclosed in the article.

**Bearing:** C3 (AI content is currently elective-only — MECM90048 "AI and Communication Science," JOUR90029 — never reaching a compulsory or capstone subject). This theme is direct evidence that leaving AI treatment elective-only is a real curricular gap relative to what comparable publishing programs elsewhere are already doing.

### Theme 2 — where AI is furthest along in adjacent creative-industry work is the mechanical, high-volume correction and documentation layer, not judgement-based editorial or curatorial work

A Stanford-led audit of AI agent automation potential across 104 US occupations classified "Proofreaders and Copy Markers" as one of 16 occupations predominantly rated H1 on the Human Agency Scale — the category denoting the lowest required level of human involvement ([Future of Work with AI Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06576), arXiv, 2025-06-06). Separately, the American Alliance of Museums' member magazine reported that archivists at the B&O Railroad Museum used an AI system built with Johns Hopkins University to perform optical character recognition and demographic-data extraction across 16 million Relief Department records, with archivists' role shifting toward reviewing AI-generated transcriptions rather than performing manual transcription ([Unlocking the Past with AI at the B&O Railroad Museum](https://www.aam-us.org/2025/06/29/unlocking-the-past-with-ai-at-the-bo-railroad-museum/), American Alliance of Museums, 2025-06-29). The Art Newspaper reported that an AI-generated digital-mask technique cut the manual in-painting time for restoring one heavily damaged painting from many months to several hours, with its developer describing it as "a tool in the toolbox" rather than a replacement for conservators ([Mechanical engineer develops AI-generated digital masks to restore damaged paintings](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2025/07/04/mechanical-engineer-develops-ai-generated-digital-masks-to-restore-damaged-paintings), The Art Newspaper, 2025-07-04).

This supports a claim that mechanical correction, transcription and restoration tasks are where AI is concentrating in adjacent occupations; it does not establish routine practice-wide displacement in Australian publishing or collections roles — the Stanford figure is a US-only expert-panel classification, the AAM piece is one institution's own account, and the Art Newspaper case is one demonstrated example, not a survey.

**Bearing:** C2 (currently Level 2 — appraisal and critique are assessed, but no item requires students to justify reliance on AI output or defend a judgement of AI-generated quality, which would be needed for Level 3) and C3 (AI treatment elective-only). Together these sources make the empirical case that judging and validating AI output — not just producing text without it — is where the destination occupations are already moving, which is exactly the gap C2 and C3 identify.

### Theme 3 — editorial and conservation work is currently governed as human labour under existing industrial and professional-ethics instruments, with no AI-specific carve-out yet

The Fair Work Commission's Book Industry Award 2020 (MA000078), consolidated to 1 July 2026, places proofreading duties within the Level 1 Trainee Book Editor classification in Schedule A ([Book Industry Award 2020](https://awards.fairwork.gov.au/MA000078.html), Fair Work Ombudsman/Fair Work Commission, consolidated 2026-07-01). The Australian Institute for the Conservation of Cultural Material binds its members to a Code of Ethics and Code of Practice first adopted in 1987 ([Code of Ethics and Code of Practice](https://aiccm.org.au/about/code-ethics-and-practice/), AICCM, adopted 1987).

This supports a claim about how entry-level editorial work and conservation practice are currently classified and governed as human labour under Australian industrial and professional-body instruments; it says nothing about whether or how quickly that classification might change as AI tools spread, and is not itself AI-adoption evidence.

**Bearing:** G1 (discipline-specificity gate, currently PASS — compulsory subjects and all three capstone streams are publishing-specific, not generic) and C1 (collaboration is currently capstone-only, Level 1). These instruments describe the human-labour framing the program's compulsory subjects are training graduates toward, which is the backdrop against which G1's pass and C1's gap should be read.

## 5. CURRICULUM IMPLICATIONS

| # | Implication | Dimension | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| CI-1 | C3 sits at Level 1 because AI content (MECM90048, JOUR90029) is confined to electives, while Theme 1 shows peer publishing-education programs (NYU Center for Publishing, Portland State University) are already embedding AI-literacy directly into copyediting and developmental-editing coursework. | D3 Technical/AI currency | Move an AI-tool-literacy component into a compulsory unit (PUBL90001 Structural Editing or PUBL90002 Copyediting) rather than leaving it elective-only. |
| CI-2 | C2 is Level 2 (appraisal/critique is assessed) but has no item requiring students to justify reliance on AI output or defend a judgement of AI-generated quality — exactly the skill Theme 2 shows is rising fastest in the destination occupations (proofreader H1 rating, archivist AI-output review). | D2 Judgement under AI assistance | Add an assessed task in a compulsory editing unit requiring students to critique AI-generated edits/copy and justify accept-or-reject decisions. |
| CI-3 | C1 and W1 are capstone-only (Level 1): collaborative and professional-genre practice reaches only students who choose the Industry Project or WIL capstone route, not the Minor Thesis route, even though entry-level editorial work is classified at trainee level under the Book Industry Award (Theme 3). | D5 Program-wide assured-skills coverage | Require the professional-genre/team deliverable (blogpost, book proposal, or handover report) in at least one compulsory unit, not only in capstone electives. |

### Critical evidence gaps to close before institutional use:

1. No Australia-specific, occupation-verified job-ad count exists for proofreaders/copy markers or museum technicians/conservators — the templated `jobAds` block in those two records should be replaced with a genuine Adzuna query before this report is used for resourcing decisions.
2. PUBL90003, compulsory for the 150- and 200-point program variants, has no captured subject or assessment page; several curriculum-evidence scores that currently rest only on PUBL90001/PUBL90002 could shift once it is verified.
3. The Publishers Weekly 63%-AI-adoption figure has no disclosed survey methodology or sample size; corroborate against a primary survey source before citing it as an occupation-wide adoption rate.
4. No targeted Australia-specific discourse sample (a deliberate LinkedIn/X search on publishing or editorial AI use) was performed for this report — the generic 30-day harvest present in the underlying profession records is self-selected and largely off-topic to publishing, so it was not used as theme evidence here.
