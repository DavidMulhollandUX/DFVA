## 6. EVIDENCE CONFIDENCE & LIMITATIONS — Basis: reported

**Panel A metadata.** Basis tier **field** (field grain), n = 0, 14 destination titles, 4 at medium mapping confidence. Index variant AIOE-2021; destination titles mapped to the published Felten index. Basis note: field-of-education occupation list (ATO-linked), not this program's own graduates

**Panel C provenance.** Scored from the 2026 handbook capture (19 pages) and verified on 2026-08-29: adversarial refutation of every level-3 score and every gate PASS (run), verbatim evidence check (run), and the construct-boundary pass.

**Ambiguities and construct-boundary notes** (7):

- C1: EDUC90194's assessed group oral presentation demonstrates genuine collaborative assessment, but it is an elective (12.5cp choice among four) not a core unit, so it was not used to raise the score above level 1.
- C2: EDUC90846's capstone report ('reflecting on their development as an evidence-based practitioner') is graded and could be read as assessed self-appraisal (level 2), but the extract does not show it is judged against explicit quality-appraisal criteria; resolved to level 1.
- C4: Stream B's capstone (EDUC90334/90335) is a genuinely self-scoped research project matching the level-3 anchor, but this route is not taken by Stream A students, who instead do supervised case-study practicum; resolved to level 2.
- C5: Stream A's case-study capstone clearly meets level 2 (real data collection, assessed methodology); Stream B's parallel capstone is a literature review plus research proposal only, with no primary data collection, closer to level 1; the program score follows Stream A's stronger route but is not lifted to level 3 because that route is not the only one.
- W1: The capstone report 'in the style of a journal article' and the mini-conference presentations gesture toward a professional genre and an audience beyond the teaching team (level 2), but the extract never states the audience is external or that marking criteria are drawn from professional practice; resolved to level 1.
- W2: Stream A's capstone practicum/case-study task is a strong, arguably level-3, authentic task (real learner, real resource/time limits, supervised, structured deadlines), but the uncaptured specialisation-core subjects and Stream B's weaker parallel route mean 'assessment spine across the program' cannot be confirmed from this extract; resolved to level 2.
- W3: Stream A delivers a required, supervised, assessed 30-day practicum sequence matching level 3's language closely, but Stream B explicitly substitutes two research-based minor projects for the professional experience, so the requirement is not universal across the cohort; resolved to level 2.

**Not scoreable from the handbook** (2):

- The five compulsory specialisation-core subjects (62.5 of 100 credit points, common to the Deaf Education / Disability / Learning Difficulties specialisations) are not present in this extract — the course-structure page defers to a 'specialisation page in Major, Minors and specialisations' that was not captured. All scores above rely only on the course-level overview/outcomes pages, the four listed elective subjects, and both capstone streams; item-level detail for the specialisation core itself is not scoreable from this extract.
- C3's level-0 score depends on an absence of AI/digital content across the captured pages; if the uncaptured specialisation-core subjects contain such content, it is not reflected in this score.

**Standing v4 limitations.**

- Scores describe **documented curriculum intent**, not demonstrated graduate
  capability; the extrapolation warrant is constructive alignment, stated as an
  assumption (Kane [17]).
- Indigenous data governance is not scored as a distinct construct; C3 level 3
  counts it where taught (CARE Principles; Lodge et al. 2025 Principle 4 [[2]](https://www.acses.edu.au/publication/australian-framework-for-artificial-intelligence-in-higher-education/)).
- Perception/manipulation (Frey & Osborne's third bottleneck [[7]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.techfore.2016.08.019)) is unscored.
- A high W3 score describes curriculum design, not student welfare: required
  placements can disadvantage students who cannot afford unpaid work or
  relocation (Accord [[30]](https://www.education.gov.au/australian-universities-accord/resources/final-report); Fawns et al. [[24]](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2024.2404634) on ableism and elitism).
- W2 is scored against each discipline's own criterion situation [[22]](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02504676), so
  cross-disciplinary W2 comparison is weaker than within-discipline comparison.
- W3 excludes simulation by design (simulation scores in W2), so programs whose
  professional norm is high-fidelity simulation may score low on W3 for a
  defensible pedagogic choice. Read the two sub-scores together.
- The authentic-assessment employability evidence rests largely on student
  self-report [[25]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.stueduc.2021.101030); W2's warrant is documented design fidelity plus the
  assessment-drives-learning mechanism [26], not demonstrated employment effects.
- Work-integrated learning is NOT claimed to raise employment rates — Jackson &
  Collings [[28]](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10734-017-0216-z) found it does not. W3's warrant is skill development, employment
  relevance and professional identity formation [[29]](https://doi.org/10.1080/07294360.2016.1139551).
