**Current:** 24/36 MODERATE RISK (v1 composite, superseded — file metadata only, not rendered) | **Duration:** 36 months full-time (300 credit points)
**v3.1 Position:** High exposure · low adaptiveness — Destination AI Exposure 94.43 (measured, portfolio median 90.9) × Curriculum Adaptiveness 9/15 (scored, median 10) · position confidence: near a threshold (81%)
**Goal:** Move the scored axis past the adaptiveness threshold — close the two core gaps (D3 Technical Depth 1/3, D5 AI Literacy 2/3), restore the D4 decision-making gate, and steer graduates toward the growing AI-adjacent tier of legal work.

---

## 1. DIAGNOSTIC SUMMARY

Under the v3.1 durability assessment the Juris Doctor sits at **High exposure · low adaptiveness**: its graduates enter some of the most AI-exposed destinations in the portfolio (exposure **94.43** against a median of 90.9 — driven by solicitor, legal counsel and paralegal destinations whose research, drafting and review tasks overlap heavily with current legal AI), while the curriculum's scored defences sit below the portfolio median (adaptiveness **9/15**, median 10). High exposure is not a finding that these jobs are disappearing — it says the *content* of the work is changing. The concern is narrower and fixable: the compulsory core contains **no AI literacy content** and **no quantitative or empirical methods requirement**; both exist only as electives most graduates never take. The program's regulatory moat (VLAB accreditation) and its strong advocacy and client-relational training are genuine, durable protections.

The position sits **one point from the adaptiveness threshold**, so it should be read as approximate — and it also means a small number of real curriculum changes moves it.

## 7. ASSESSMENT REDESIGN EXAMPLES

1. **AI-Augmented Contract Review Exercise (replacing a traditional contract-drafting exam question):** Students are given an AI-generated first-pass contract review (with deliberately seeded errors and omissions) and must identify, correct, and justify changes, then advise a simulated client on residual risk — building the AI-supervision capability (D5) the exposed destinations now demand.
2. **Empirical Legal Research Mini-Project (extending the Legal Research capstone):** Instead of purely doctrinal research, students design a small empirical study (e.g., analysing a sample of judgments or tribunal decisions for a pattern) using basic data-literacy skills from the new legal-analytics unit, then defend their methodology — assessing D3 and D7 together.
3. **AI Governance Advisory Simulation (within the compulsory AI literacy module):** Students role-play as in-house counsel advising a client organisation on deploying an AI system, requiring them to identify legal, ethical, and governance risks and draft a short advisory memo — assessing D5 alongside the decision-making capability the D4 gate requires.

## 8. AI GOVERNANCE AND QUALITY CONTROLS

* Require disclosure and critical review of any AI tool used in coursework, modelling the professional obligation lawyers will face when using AI in practice (aligns compulsory AI literacy training with real professional-conduct expectations).
* Ensure the compulsory AI literacy module explicitly covers AI's failure modes in legal contexts (hallucinated citations, biased outputs, confidentiality/privilege risks) — not just tool proficiency.
* Establish a faculty advisory input loop with practising lawyers using legal AI tools (per P1/P4) to keep AI-related curriculum content current against a fast-moving legal-AI product landscape.
* Maintain VLAB accreditation compliance as a hard constraint on any curriculum change — new AI/analytics content must be additive to, not a replacement for, the Priestley 11 compulsory areas.

## 10. RISKS, TRADE-OFFS, AND DEPENDENCIES

* **Risk: the position is misread as a verdict.** The assessment says exposed destinations plus below-threshold curriculum defences — not that legal jobs are disappearing (economy-wide, the most AI-exposed occupations are projected to grow) and not that the program is failing. *Mitigation:* every communication pairs the exposure figure with the growth framing and the specific, fixable gaps.
* **Risk: threshold proximity cuts both ways.** The position sits one point from the adaptiveness median, so a single rating difference — or portfolio drift — can move the quadrant without any real change. *Mitigation:* track the raw scores (adaptiveness, per-item) and gate states, not the quadrant label.
* **Risk: VLAB accreditation constraints limit how much compulsory-core content can change without disrupting Priestley 11 coverage.** *Mitigation:* Design new AI literacy and analytics content as integrated additions within existing compulsory subjects (e.g., embedded modules) rather than requiring net-new standalone subjects that add credit-point pressure.
* **Risk: Faculty staffing capacity for AI/tech-forward electives is already strained, evidenced by 15+ electives unavailable in 2026.** *Mitigation:* Prioritise stabilising existing AI-elective delivery (P3) before launching the new specialisation track (P4), sequencing effort to avoid overcommitting scarce specialist staff.
* **Risk: Fast-moving legal AI product landscape (Harvey, CoCounsel, Lexis+ AI, etc.) could make specific tool-based content stale within a year or two.** *Mitigation:* Frame the compulsory AI literacy module around durable governance/critical-evaluation principles rather than any single vendor tool, with an annual content refresh cycle tied to the faculty advisory panel.

## 11. REDESIGNED GRADUATE PROFILE (2027 READY)

The 2027-ready Melbourne JD graduate holds the same VLAB-accredited admission to practice and the same depth across the Priestley 11 areas that anchor the program's domain expertise and human/relational training today — but arrives with AI literacy and basic empirical/analytical capability built into the core, not left to chance via elective choice. This graduate can supervise and critically evaluate AI-assisted contract review and legal research output rather than simply consuming it, can reason about a legal problem using both doctrinal and empirical methods, and can advise clients on AI governance risk as competently as on any Priestley 11 subject area. They enter the most AI-exposed destinations in the portfolio carrying the defences the assessment measures — and a growing share of them enter the legal-technology and AI-governance tier where the exposure is the opportunity, not the pressure.
