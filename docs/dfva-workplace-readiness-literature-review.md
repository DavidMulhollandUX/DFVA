# Workplace Readiness — Literature Review for Panel C v4.1

**Prompted by:** A/Prof Kayley Lyons's review of the Panel C v4 draft, August 2026 —
*"It's looking closer now. I agree with those factors as the ones that will make a student
more successful in an AI world. Keep those in. Just need to also add in factors that make a
student be successful in any workplace including AI ones… For example, the content has a focus
on professional skills (eg teamwork), deeper learning approaches (eg placements), authentic
assessments."*

## 1. What the feedback identifies

The v4 review found the AI-durability half of the construct and re-anchored it on TEQSA's four
adaptive capabilities (Lodge et al. 2026). That work is endorsed and stands: *"keep those in."*

The new finding is a **second construct-underrepresentation defect**, orthogonal to the first.
v4 asks what makes a graduate durable *against AI substitution*. It never asks what makes a
graduate employable *at all* — the capabilities every workplace has always required, which an
AI-integrated workplace still requires and in several respects requires more of. Three examples
are named:

| Named in the feedback | Construct family | Status in v4 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| professional skills (e.g. teamwork) | generic employment-related capability | partial — teamwork enters C1 only as *distributed cognition*, i.e. as an AI-coupling construct |
| deeper learning approaches (e.g. placements) | pedagogic form / work-situated learning | absent as a construct; WIL appears only as one exemplar inside a C4 anchor |
| authentic assessments | assessment design fidelity | absent; the word does not occur in the instrument |

This is the same defect class the v4 review named, applied to a different region of the
construct space: *"construct underrepresentation and method bias"* (Woods, Lyons et al. 2026).
It is worth stating plainly that the omission is systematic, not accidental — v4 derived its
items top-down from a single framework whose scope is, by design, AI adaptation. Anything the
TEQSA capability set does not name, v4 cannot see.

## 2. Coverage audit — what v4 actually measures, item by item

Before adding anything, the honest question is whether the gap is real or whether the existing
five items already cover it under other names. Audited against the feedback's three families:

| v4 item | Covers | Does **not** cover |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C1 Distributed cognition | teamwork *where it is coupled to tools/AI systems*; coordination of roles | communicating to non-specialist, client or public audiences; professional conduct, reliability, accountability to someone other than an examiner |
| C2 Hybrid metacognition | evaluative judgement, reliance decisions | — (this item is the one place v4 and the authentic-assessment literature already agree; see §4.3) |
| C3 Digital & AI literacy | AI capability, limits, governance | non-AI professional technology practice (scored, at most, as level 1 tool operation) |
| C4 Life-long learning & transfer | transfer to novel problems, self-directed learning | WIL is named in one level-3 exemplar, so a placement can raise C4 — but WIL is not itself scored, and a program with a full clinical placement year and a program with a self-scoped capstone score identically |
| C5 Inquiry | primary evidence generation | — |
| G1 / G2 | disciplinary depth; decision-making under uncertainty | both are gates, so neither differentiates |

The gap is real. Two of v4's items also **leak** into the missing constructs (C1 into placement
supervision, C4 into WIL), which means the current instrument scores work-situated learning
inconsistently — sometimes, through the side door, under an item that is about something else.
That is the double-counting pathology that retired the Irreplaceability bonus (LR §2.1
defect 4), reappearing in a new place. §4 fixes it.

## 3. Candidate frameworks, tiered by evidential status

Same tiering discipline as LR §3: regulator-endorsed and Australian outranks international
practitioner consensus, which outranks single studies. Sources marked ✔ were verified against
full text, not against abstracts or summaries.

### Tier 1 — Australian regulatory and national instruments

**Higher Education Standards Framework (Threshold Standards) 2021 ✔** — the strongest available
anchor, and the elegant one: it is *the same regulator* that supplies v4's construct authority.
TEQSA's adaptive-capabilities guidance is advisory; the Threshold Standards are law, and they
already require of every accredited course exactly what the feedback asks the instrument to
measure. Clause 1.4.2 (verbatim, from the registered instrument F2021L00488):

> The specified learning outcomes for each course of study encompass discipline-related and
> generic outcomes, including: … b. generic skills and their application in the context of the
> field(s) of education or disciplines involved; c. knowledge and skills required for employment
> and further study related to the course of study, including those required to be eligible to
> seek registration to practise where applicable, and d. skills in independent and critical
> thinking suitable for life-long learning.

Three things follow. First, 1.4.2(b) settles the framing question that has dogged generic-skill
measurement since Barrie: the Standards do not ask for free-floating generic skills, they ask
for generic skills **"and their application in the context of the field(s) of education or
disciplines involved"** — discipline-situated by regulation. Second, 1.4.2(c) makes
employment-related capability a *learning outcome* requirement, not a career-services add-on.
Third, clause 1.4.3 requires that *"[m]ethods of assessment are consistent with the learning
outcomes being assessed, [and] are capable of confirming that all specified learning outcomes
are achieved"* — which is the regulatory statement of the v4 design rule R2 (assessment
evidence, not outcome statements). R2 was until now justified only by constructive-alignment
theory; it is in fact a threshold standard.

Clause 5.4.1 covers the pedagogic form directly: *"Work-integrated learning, placements, other
community-based learning and collaborative research training arrangements are quality assured,
including assurance of the quality of supervision of student experiences."* WIL is a named,
separately regulated category of provision with a supervision requirement — which is what makes
it handbook-visible and therefore scoreable.

**QILT Employer Satisfaction Survey (ESS)** — the national instrument that asks employers,
rather than educators, what a graduate must be able to do. Its five attribute domains, with
published definitions: **Foundation skills** (general literacy, numeracy and communication, and
the ability to investigate and integrate knowledge); **Adaptive skills** (adapt and apply
skills/knowledge, work independently); **Collaborative skills** (teamwork and interpersonal
skills); **Technical skills** (application of professional and technical knowledge and
standards, including ethical practice); **Employability skills** (perform and innovate in the
workplace — pressure management, deadlines, organisational understanding, leadership, initiative,
responsibility for professional development).

Two observations matter for item design. (a) The ESS structure is almost the complement of
v4: Collaborative, Foundation and Employability are exactly the domains v4 does not score.
(b) In the 2025 national results the domains do **not** move together — technical skills 94.0%
and foundation skills 93.3% against collaborative 88.6% and employability 86.2%. Employers
separate technical competence from workplace conduct, and rate the latter lower. An instrument
that scores only the former will systematically overstate a program.

**Core Skills for Work Developmental Framework (Australian Government, 2013)** — the national
non-technical skills framework: three clusters (*Navigate the world of work*, *Interact with
others*, *Get the work done*), ten skill areas, five performance stages from novice to expert.
Its value here is the cluster architecture, which cleanly separates communication and
interpersonal practice ("Communicate for work", "Connect and work with others") from task
execution ("Plan and organise", "Make decisions"). Caveat: it was authored for VET and
competency-based training, so it is a content source for the anchors, not a construct authority
for higher education.

**Australian Universities Accord Final Report (2024)** — 47 recommendations; WIL is treated as
core national policy, with the Commonwealth Prac Payment following in the 2024–25 Budget for
mandatory placements in teaching, nursing and social work. This establishes WIL as a
system-level priority; it also flags the equity problem (placement poverty) that §7 records as
a declared limitation.

### Tier 2 — assessment-design constructs with operational frameworks

**Gulikers, Bastiaens & Kirschner (2004) ✔**, *ETR&D* 52(3):67–86 — the five-dimensional
framework: (a) the assessment **task**, (b) the **physical context**, (c) the **social
context**, (d) the **assessment result or form**, (e) the **criteria**. Two findings are
load-bearing for a rubric:

> These dimensions can vary in their level of authenticity (i.e., they are continuums). It is a
> misconception to think that something is either authentic or not authentic … because the
> degree of authenticity is not solely a characteristic of the assessment chosen; it needs to be
> defined in relation to the criterion situation derived from professional practice.

Authenticity is (i) a continuum — so a 0–3 anchored item is the right instrument shape — and
(ii) *relative to the discipline's own criterion situation* — so the anchors must never name a
fixed exemplar (a "client report") as the standard. Their own example: assessing in a team is
authentic only if the real task is done in a team.

**Villarroel, Bloxham, Bruna, Bruna & Herrera-Seda (2018) ✔**, *A&EHE* 43(5):840–854 — a
systematic review of 1988–2015 literature yielding three dimensions: **realism**, **cognitive
challenge**, **evaluative judgement**, plus a four-step course-design blueprint. Realism has two
forms — a real context that frames the problem, and performance-based tasks where students
produce the artefact a practitioner produces. Note the third dimension: **evaluative judgement
is already v4's C2**, on the same underlying Deakin/CRADLE literature (Tai et al. 2018). This is
a convergence, not a duplication risk — provided the new item is scoped to exclude it (§4.3).

**Sokhanvar, Salehi & Sokhanvar (2021)**, *Studies in Educational Evaluation* 70:101030 —
systematic review of 26 studies (2010–2019) on authentic assessment and employability. Finding:
authentic assessment improves engagement, satisfaction and self-reported employability skills.
Honest reading of the evidence grade: the outcome measures are predominantly student
self-report and course-level, not employment outcomes.

**Gibbs & Simpson (2004)**, *LATHE* 1:3–31 — eleven conditions under which assessment supports
learning. The mechanism claim the whole v4.1 addition rests on: assessment governs what and how
students study, so assessment design, not curriculum statement, is where a pedagogic intention
becomes a student behaviour. This is the same warrant as R2, extended from "is it assessed?" to
"what kind of studying does the assessment induce?"

### Tier 3 — pedagogic form and the deeper-learning evidence

**Kuh (2008)**, AAC&U — High-Impact Practices. Ten named practices (including internships,
capstones, undergraduate research, collaborative assignments, service/community-based learning)
whose shared features are: significant time and effort, interaction with faculty and peers about
substantive matters, contact with difference, frequent feedback, and application in novel
settings. NSSE analyses link participation to gains in deep approaches to learning and reported
learning outcomes. This is the closest thing to a construct definition for "deeper learning
approaches" as the feedback uses the phrase, and it is the reason W3 scores *form and depth of
participation*, not merely presence.

**NRC / Pellegrino & Hilton (2012)** — already reference [9] in v4, cited there for transfer.
Its deeper-learning definition (the process producing transferable knowledge across cognitive,
intrapersonal and interpersonal competency clusters) does double duty here: the
**interpersonal** cluster is the one v4 left unscored.

**Jackson & Collings (2018)**, *Higher Education* 76:403–425 — the honest counterweight, and the
reason W3's justification must be stated carefully. In two Australian samples (N = 628, N = 237)
linking institutional WIL records to national graduate outcomes data, **WIL participation did not
raise full-time employment rates**. There was a small significant difference in the first four
months, and evidence of better *quality and relevance* of employment. The defensible claim is
therefore: WIL is an evidenced deeper-learning form associated with skill development, employment
relevance and professional identity — *not* a device that raises employment rates. A
durability instrument that implied otherwise would be overclaiming, and Panel D would not
support it.

**Jackson (2016)**, *HERD* 35(5):925–939 — pre-professional identity: *"an understanding of and
connection with the skills, qualities, conduct, culture and ideology of a student's intended
profession"*, developed through participation in communities of practice. This supplies the
mechanism that distinguishes work-situated learning from a well-designed simulation: membership
and accountability in a real professional community, which is what a placement uniquely provides
and a classroom task cannot simulate.

### Tier 4 — labour-side evidence that the generic half is not soft

**Heckman, Stixrud & Urzua (2006)**, *JOLE* 24(3):411–482 — cognitive and non-cognitive
abilities jointly determine schooling, occupational choice, wages and social behaviour; for many
outcomes the two are comparably important. **Deming (2017)** is already reference [5] in v4, cited
for C1; its Figure IV result (social-skill-intensive employment +11.8 pp of the US workforce
1980–2012 while math-intensive/low-social work shrank) is at least as strong a warrant for a
professional-practice item as for a distributed-cognition one.

**Tomlinson (2017)**, *Education + Training* 59(4):338–352 — graduate capital: human, social,
cultural, identity and psychological. Used here only as a map of what a curriculum-side
instrument *cannot* reach: social and cultural capital are largely not curriculum properties.
This is a scoping source, not an item source.

### Counter-evidence — the sources that constrain the design

**Fawns, Bearman, Dawson, Nieminen, Ashford-Rowe, Willey, Jensen, Damşa & Press (2024) ✔**,
*A&EHE* 50(3):396–408 — the strongest constraint on a naive authenticity item, and from the same
group whose evaluative-judgement work grounds C2:

> Despite literature supporting its potential benefits, there is limited evidence on the
> relationship between authentic assessment and these challenges. Through an uncritical blending
> of authenticity with broader educational goals, the label 'authentic assessment' risks becoming
> a distraction or a thought-terminating cliché.

Their prescription — treat authenticity as *"a set of aspirational principles within a broader
pedagogical framework"*, requiring contextualised design and negotiated trade-offs — translates
directly into a scoring rule: score documented task features, never the label. They also note
that some forms of authenticity (long clinical shifts, unpaid placements) *"could be seen as
ableist or elitist"* — which is a limitation the instrument must declare rather than encode as
unqualified virtue.

**Barrie (2006, 2007)** — the graduate-attribute critique: institutions assert generic attributes
but *"do not normally measure"* their attainment, and academics hold four incompatible
understandings of what they even are, from generic precursor skills through to abilities
interwoven with disciplinary scholarship. Two consequences: (a) a program's stated graduate
attributes are worthless as evidence — which R2 already handles; (b) generic-skill anchors must
be written in discipline-situated terms, which HESF 1.4.2(b) independently requires.

**Equity in WIL** — participation and outcomes differ across student groups, and the Accord's
placement-poverty finding is the policy expression of the same problem. A program scoring 3 on a
mandatory unpaid placement item is not unambiguously better for all its students. Declared in §7.

## 4. What the review concludes for the instrument

### 4.1 The gap is a second sub-construct, not five more items

The feedback's three families are not scattered additions to the adaptive-capability set; they
share a construct: **the capabilities and curriculum forms that make a graduate effective in a
workplace, whatever technology that workplace runs on.** They have their own authority (HESF
1.4.2/5.4.1, ESS, CSfW), their own evidence base, and — critically — a different theory of
change from the adaptive capabilities. Merging them into C1–C5 would recreate exactly the defect
this review found: one construct's items silently absorbing another's content.

The recommended shape is therefore **two named sub-scales inside Panel C**, not a longer flat
list: Adaptive capabilities (C1–C5, /15, unchanged) and Workplace practice (W1–W3, /9, new).
The full argument and the anchors are in the [v4.1 recommendation](dfva-panelc-v41-recommendation.md).

### 4.2 Three items, cut where the literature cuts

- **W1 · Professional communication and conduct** — HESF 1.4.2(b)(c); ESS Foundation +
  Collaborative + Employability domains; CSfW cluster 2; NACE Communication/Professionalism/
  Teamwork. The construct is *communicating and conducting oneself as a professional with people
  who are not your examiner*.
- **W2 · Authentic task design** — Gulikers's five dimensions and Villarroel's realism and
  cognitive challenge, scored relative to the discipline's own criterion situation.
- **W3 · Work-situated learning** — HESF 5.4.1; Kuh's HIPs; Jackson's pre-professional identity;
  Accord. Scored on *depth of participation in a real professional community*, which is the
  feature simulation cannot substitute for.

W2 and W3 are separable and must stay separate: W2 is available to every program regardless of
placement capacity (a fully classroom-based program can score 3), and W3 requires an external
host. Collapsing them would make the item a proxy for discipline type rather than curriculum
design.

### 4.3 The three overlaps that must be closed in the same change

Adding items without re-cutting the existing ones would reintroduce halo. Three edits:

1. **C1 level 3** currently offers *"supervised placements with multidisciplinary
   accountability"* as an exemplar. That is now W3's construct. C1's level 3 must be
   human-plus-tool coordination only.
2. **C4 level 3** currently offers *"work-integrated learning requiring performance in a context
   not taught"* as one of three routes. Same problem; remove the WIL route, leaving transfer and
   self-directed learning.
3. **W2 must explicitly exclude evaluative judgement**, Villarroel's third dimension, because it
   is C2. W2 scores realism, cognitive challenge and practice-derived criteria; the appraisal
   capability itself stays in C2.

After these edits every construct has exactly one home, which is the property that the retirement
of the Irreplaceability bonus was meant to establish and that this review would otherwise break.

### 4.4 The cost, stated

Panel C becomes /24 (15 + 9). This breaks the /15 scale that the v4 recommendation §3 deliberately
protected — the one thing v4 promised not to do. The protection was for the exact-enumeration
stability layer, and that layer survives: 3⁸ = 6,561 perturbation states against v3.1's 3⁵ = 243.
Both are trivially enumerable; the algorithm is unchanged and only the state space grows. What
genuinely changes is every display surface, the reference medians, and the migration table — all
of which the §4 migration cycle already has to redo for v4 anyway. Doing both re-scalings in one
published cycle costs less than doing them in two.

## 5. What this buys on the validity argument

The v4 validation sequence (recommendation §5, ordered on Kane 2013) improves in three places:

| Step | Effect of v4.1 |
| --- | --- |
| 2 · Content validity | The content panel now has a *complete* construct to rate. Under v4 a reviewer asked "is anything missing?" would find the same gap Lyons found; the CVI would be measuring a known-incomplete instrument. |
| 5 · Internal structure | The two sub-scales make a **testable prediction**: C1–C5 and W1–W3 should resolve as two correlated factors, not one. That is a falsifiable structural hypothesis, which the flat five-item v4 could not offer. If they collapse into one factor, the sub-scale distinction is wrong and should be abandoned. |
| 6 · Extrapolation | W-items connect to an external criterion v4 had no route to: the ESS domains are measured nationally, by employers, on graduates of these institutions. The prediction that W-subscale scores relate to ESS Collaborative/Employability domain ratings is checkable at the field-of-education grain. |

There is also a lower-cost gain: the content-validity ask to Lyons is now demonstrably *her own
review, implemented*, which is a materially stronger opening than the v4 draft alone.

## 6. What the review does not support

- **Scoring "employability" as a whole.** Employability models (CareerEDGE; Tomlinson's capital
  model) include self-efficacy, emotional intelligence, career self-management and social capital.
  These are student properties and network properties, not curriculum properties, and no handbook
  documents them. The instrument scores curriculum; the review's scoping conclusion is that the
  employability literature's curriculum-visible residue is exactly W1–W3.
- **Claiming WIL improves employment rates.** Jackson & Collings (2018) is direct evidence against
  it. W3's warrant is skill development, employment relevance and professional identity formation.
- **Treating "authentic" as a virtue label.** Fawns et al. (2024) is explicit; the anchors score
  documented task features only, and a new design rule R4 makes that binding.
- **A fourth item for self-management / initiative.** ESS's Employability domain and CSfW's "Get
  the work done" cluster would support one, but its handbook-visible evidence (deadlines, project
  management, individual accountability) is largely the same evidence W1 and W2 already cite.
  Excluded on parsimony and non-overlap; revisit if the content panel disagrees.

## 7. Declared limitations (stated here, carried into the methodology note)

1. **Equity of the W3 anchors.** Required placements can disadvantage students who cannot afford
   unpaid work or relocation (Accord; Fawns et al. on ableism/elitism). A high W3 score is a
   statement about curriculum design, not about student welfare, and the reports must say so.
2. **Discipline-relativity of authenticity.** W2 is defined against the discipline's own criterion
   situation (Gulikers). Cross-disciplinary comparison of W2 is therefore weaker than
   within-discipline comparison — the same caveat that already applies to C5.
3. **Self-report ceiling in the authentic-assessment evidence.** Sokhanvar et al.'s employability
   findings rest largely on student self-report; the W2 warrant is design fidelity plus the
   Gibbs & Simpson mechanism, not demonstrated employment effects.
4. **Simulation vs situation.** W3 excludes simulation deliberately (it is W2). Programs in
   disciplines where high-fidelity simulation is the professional norm may be scored low on W3
   for a defensible pedagogic choice. The two sub-scores must be read together, never summed
   into a single "workplace" impression.
5. **CSfW provenance.** Used as an anchor-content source only; it is a VET competency framework,
   not a higher-education construct authority.
