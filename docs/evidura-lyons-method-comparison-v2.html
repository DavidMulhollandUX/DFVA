Method comparison: competency mapping and DFVA v3.1

A structural comparison of the competency-mapping method reported in Mun, Chanchlani, Lyons & Gray (2024) with the DFVA v3.1 instrument. The comparison sets out what each method takes as input, what operation it performs, and what it treats as a possible source of error.

01 — THE TWO METHODS

Two procedures for assessing a curriculum against a reference

Both methods reduce a curriculum to structured statements, compare those statements against a reference, and produce a judgment about the program's position. They differ in what each treats as the likely source of error, and therefore in what each does to guard against it.

Method B — DFVA v3.1
August 2026 · amends v3 §4, §5.2, §7, §8 · 34 placed programs
PANEL A — MEASURED
Destination exposure
  • Mean Felten AI Occupational Exposure across the occupations graduates enter
  • Destination titles drawn from JIR/LiveAlumni data at program grain
  • Crosswalk of 368 titles to 106 O*NET-SOC codes, a ratio of 3.47 to 1

  • Provenance is a required machine-checked field
PANEL C — SCORED
Curriculum adaptiveness
  • Five items, each scored 0..3: systems thinking, technical depth, research methods, irreplaceability, AI literacy
  • Scored from handbook and syllabus text
  • No external competency framework is used as a reference
  • No inter-rater study has been conducted (open item R9)
UNCERTAINTY
Exact enumeration
  • All 3⁵ = 243 perturbation states are enumerated rather than sampled
  • The Monte-Carlo procedure and its seed were removed; output is identical between runs
  • Computed at three assumed rater-error rates: e = 0.05, 0.10, 0.20
  • Each distribution must sum to 1 within 1e-12 or the build fails

02 — POINTS OF COMPLEMENTARITY

Each method addresses a limitation present in the other

The two methods have non-overlapping weaknesses. Method B quantifies rating uncertainty but assumes the rate at which raters err; Method A obtains expert agreement but does not report what that agreement was or what would follow from disagreement.

Method B: the error rate is assumed rather than measured

The uncertainty layer in v3.1 is parameterised by e, the per-item rater-error rate. The specification states this directly:

“No inter-rater study has been run, so none of the three is empirically grounded (v3 open item R9). Publishing only the middle column presents a choice of assumption as a property of the portfolio.”

An expert panel independently confirming a mapping, as used in stage 2 of Method A, is one procedure by which e could be estimated from data.

Method A: no uncertainty is reported

The mapping is reported as settled. No rater-agreement statistic, sensitivity analysis, or account of what a second panel's disagreement would have implied is given. The limitation the authors do state is narrower:

“Consultation for this project was informed by reports that included student evaluations but mainly occurred at the faculty educators and executive level.”

The enumeration procedure in Method B is a means of identifying which mapping decisions determine the result and which do not.

The count varies by a factor of seven across the range of assumed rates. Estimating the rate from data would therefore constrain a result that is at present determined by an assumption.

03 — LIMITATIONS DOCUMENTED IN METHOD B

The modal-probability distribution is bimodal and the published threshold falls inside the lower cluster

Exact enumeration showed structure that the earlier sampling procedure had obscured. The 34 modal probabilities take 13 distinct values, forming two clusters separated by a band 0.13 wide in which no program falls. The published m ≥ 0.80 rule cuts within the lower cluster rather than between the two.

What v3.1 changed

The Monte-Carlo procedure was replaced by exact enumeration of the 243 states. The published dual-label count had previously depended on the random seed, returning 0, 1 or 2 on re-runs. It is now deterministic.

What v3.1 discloses

53 of 170 scored items, or 31 per cent, sit at the top of the scale, which makes the error model asymmetric with an expected drift of −0.075 points. A symmetric alternative was tested and no program changed label regime.

What remains open

The rater study (R9); the hybrid-crosswalk trade-off; the evidence-tier trade-off; coverage bias, since 32 of 66 programs are unplaced; and re-anchoring of the AI-literacy item.

SUMMARY

Method B applies a precise uncertainty procedure to a measurement that has not been validated. It enumerates the consequences of a rating error whose rate has not been estimated, across five items that are not anchored to an external framework.

Method A has the opposite profile: an externally anchored and expert-confirmed mapping, with no account of its own uncertainty and a refresh cycle measured in years.

Neither method is complete on its own, and the limitations are of a kind that the other addresses. Combining them is a defensible basis for a joint study.
