# Curriculum Adaptiveness — Literature Review and a Grounded Replacement Rubric

**Prompted by:** A/Prof Kayley Lyons's review of a DFVA v3.1 report, August 2026 —
*"Part B down to skill shift summary is the most useful right now. The other sections need
some conceptual work but will be useful."* Her recommended next step was a literature review
to find the best-grounded durability rubric available, and to re-anchor Curriculum
Adaptiveness on it.

## 1. What the feedback identifies

The sections she rated most useful — Part B through §4 Skill Shift Summary — are the
**observational** ones: job family map, recent job-ad signals, current discussion signals,
skill shift summary. Every claim in that run is sourced to something outside DFVA.

The sections she set aside — §5 Curriculum Implications, §6 Evidence Confidence, the
improvement plan, and Part A's scored axis — are the **inferential** ones. They are where
DFVA stops reporting evidence and starts exercising judgement through a rubric it invented.

That is the same distinction she has already published, in Woods, Lyons et al. (2026):

> *"most authors developed their own constructs, tests, and surveys **without evidence of
> validity, reliability, or generalizability**… increases the risk of multiple types of
> measurement errors, including **construct underrepresentation and method bias**."*

So the brief is not "improve the wording of the anchors". It is: give Curriculum Adaptiveness
a construct definition that exists independently of the items that measure it, and ground
those items in something a reviewer can check.

## 2. The construct as it currently stands

Panel C — Curriculum Adaptiveness — is five items scored 0–3 from handbook evidence, summed
to /15. Definition of record (v2 §4.1): *"the degree to which the curriculum builds
capabilities that resist AI substitution in exposed destinations."*

| Item | Name | Retention rationale on record (v2 §4.1) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D2 | Systems Thinking and Problem Framing | Discriminating in v1; weakly performed by current models |
| D3 | Technical and Quantitative Depth | Discriminating; substitution barrier per human-capital theory |
| D7 | Research Methods Rigour | Discriminating; direct market signal |
| B | Irreplaceability Premium | Highest item coherence in the v1 matrix (item–total r = 0.65) |
| D5 | AI Literacy and Governance | Largest portfolio gap in v1 (83% of programs ≤ 1) |

Gates: D4 (Decision-Making), D6 (Domain Depth) — saturated, recast as PASS/FAIL, excluded from
the sum. Dropped entirely: D9 (Curriculum Currency, item–total r = 0.06).

**This is not a cosmetic layer.** Adaptiveness is the y-axis of the published position, and
**9 of the 34 reference programs tie exactly at the adaptiveness median**, so the tie rule
alone decides the quadrant label for a quarter of the placed portfolio. A one-point change in
any item's definition moves real labels.

### 2.1 Four defects the literature makes visible

**Defect 1 — the construct is defined by its own items.** "Capabilities that resist AI
substitution" is not independently specified anywhere; the only operational content is the
five anchors. There is no external referent against which an item could be judged missing.
This is the precondition for defects 2–4, not a separate problem.

**Defect 2 — construct underrepresentation.** Messick's term, and the one Lyons's review uses.
Absent from Panel C entirely: metacognition and self-regulation; evaluative judgement;
transfer and continued learning; ethical reasoning; human–AI teaming; relational capability
(D8 exists in v1 and is *not* in Panel C). Section 3 shows every one of these named in a
current framework.

**Defect 3 — item selection is contradicted by the labour economics the rubric cites.**
D3 (Technical Depth) is grounded on human-capital theory as a "substitution barrier". Deming &
Noray (2020, *QJE*) is the direct test of that claim and it points the other way: the applied-
STEM wage premium falls from **44% at age 24 to 14% at age 35**, because applied technical
skill content is where obsolescence is *fastest*. Technical depth is a real asset and belongs
in the instrument — but as the disciplinary foundation, not as evidence of *adaptiveness*.
Conversely Deming (2017, *QJE*) finds social-skill-intensive employment grew ~12pp of the US
workforce 1980–2012 while math-intensive-but-less-social work shrank 3.3pp, with the strongest
growth where both were required. Panel C scores the first of those and excludes the second.

**Defect 4 — B (Irreplaceability) is a verdict, not an observable.** Its anchors ("rare
integration of technical depth, domain expertise, and human judgment") summarise D3, D6 and
D8 rather than observing anything new. Its high item–total correlation (r = 0.65) — the stated
reason for retaining it — is exactly what a general-impression item produces. In rating
research that is the signature of **halo**, and it is evidence against treating B as a distinct
dimension, not for it.

**A methodological note on how the five were chosen.** They were selected by item analysis on
v1 — discrimination and item–total correlation. That is a legitimate technique applied to an
instrument that had no validity evidence to begin with, so it optimised *spread* rather than
*meaning*. An item can discriminate beautifully between programs and still measure the wrong
thing.

## 3. Candidate frameworks, tiered by evidential status

### Tier 1 — regulator-endorsed, Australian, current, and about precisely this construct

**TEQSA / Lodge et al. (June 2026), *Assuring quality learning in a gen AI-integrated future:
The role of adaptive capabilities*.** This is the find of the review. It is eight weeks old, it
was commissioned by the national regulator, and it defines the construct DFVA has been calling
Curriculum Adaptiveness — under almost the same name.

> **Adaptive capabilities:** *"the high-level, integrated capacities that enable graduates to
> navigate complex, novel and gen AI-integrated environments. These capabilities empower
> individuals to deploy, adapt and transfer their specific, demonstrable skills effectively
> and ethically."*

Four capabilities, an explicit core, and an explicit foundation:

| Capability | Definition | Named sub-components |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Digital literacy** | Using digital tools (incl. gen AI) effectively, ethically and safely | Information & data literacy; communication & collaboration; digital content creation; protection & safety; technical proficiency & problem-solving (per the Australian Digital Capability Framework / DigComp), *extended* to critical understanding of gen AI principles, limitations, ethics, societal impact and power structures |
| **Distributed cognition** | How cognitive processes, information and tasks are shared across people, tools, artefacts and gen AI systems | Working in teams and human–gen AI collaborations; using external representations and agents to extend thinking; understanding how knowledge and tasks flow across a distributed system; coordinating roles and resources |
| **Hybrid metacognition** | Regulation of thinking and learning within any cognitive system, including human–gen AI networks | **Evaluative judgement**; monitoring and evaluating AI-generated information; deciding when to rely on or override gen AI; co-regulation; ethical reasoning about gen AI use |
| **Life-long learning** | Sustaining motivation, capability and adaptability to learn continuously in evolving, uncertain, gen AI-mediated contexts | Adapting to evolving technologies and contexts; identifying knowledge gaps and independently acquiring skills; **transferring learning across tasks, domains and tools**; sustaining motivation; learner identity grounded in agency and growth |

At the centre: **agency and regulation**. Underneath all four: **deep disciplinary knowledge**,
described as *"the epistemic foundation for authentic judgement and expertise"*, with the
explicit condition that adaptive capabilities *"need to be built on and must be learned within
the context of deep disciplinary knowledge."*

Three things make this the right anchor rather than merely a useful one:

1. **Proposition 1 is "Establish adaptive capabilities as core graduate attributes."** If the
   sector acts on that, a program-level instrument for assessing them is exactly the artefact
   institutions will need — and DFVA is already that shape.
2. It **names the AI-literacy trap DFVA's D5 sits in**: narrow, tool-focused AI literacy is
   held to be insufficient *and non-durable*, because "the operational skills associated with
   today's gen AI systems may not remain relevant as technologies evolve." D5's anchors are
   written at exactly that operational level.
3. **Four of the eighteen authors are University of Melbourne** — Jaclyn Broadbent is Deakin,
   but Sarah Frankland, Sally Male, Eduardo Oliveira and Joanne Russell are UoM. This is not a
   distant standard; it is a framework with in-house co-authors.

### 3.1 Supporting Tier 1 material (verified against full text, not summaries)

**Australian Framework for Artificial Intelligence in Higher Education** (Lodge et al., 2025) —
seven principles. Three carry direct load for DFVA:

- **Principle 1, Human-centred education**, grounds hybrid metacognition in Bearman et al.
  (2024): *"human evaluative judgement remains essential even as AI tools become increasingly
  sophisticated."* It also states plainly that designing assessment around uniquely human
  capabilities *"does not necessarily make assessment tasks secure or valid"* — a caution that
  applies equally to a rubric that scores curricula rather than students.
- **Principle 6, Fostering adaptive skills for AI integration**, is the strongest available
  confirmation of §2.1 Defect 3. It names the D5 trap explicitly: institutions should emphasise
  *"persistent skills and capabilities over skills like 'prompt engineering' that will have
  limited future utility,"* and should be *"cultivating adaptive expertise that enables students
  to transfer learning strategies across evolving sociotechnological contexts."* This is the
  Tier 2 adaptive-expertise construct (§3.2) endorsed inside a Tier 1 regulatory document —
  the two tiers are not competing options, they converge.
- **Principle 4, Indigenous knowledges**, draws on Kukutai & Taylor (2016) and the **CARE
  Principles for Indigenous Data Governance** (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control,
  Responsibility, Ethics; Carroll et al., 2020), affirming Indigenous peoples' right to control
  how their knowledges are represented in AI systems. **This is the same gap the collaborator
  brief already found by a completely different route**: Mun, Chanchlani, Lyons & Gray's (2024)
  competency mapping of UoM's Graduate Certificate surfaced Indigenous data governance as an
  uncovered domain with no home in any DFVA dimension. Two independent sources — a peer-reviewed
  curriculum mapping and a national regulatory framework — now name the same absence. It should
  be an explicit non-goal or an explicit future item in the v4 scope note, not left silent again.

**Assessment reform for the age of artificial intelligence** (Lodge, Howard, Bearman, Dawson et
al., 2023) — the founding TEQSA-commissioned paper the June 2026 report builds on, with Carlo
Perrotta (University of Melbourne) among the sixteen contributing experts. Its **Proposition 2**
is the precedent DFVA most needs:

> *"…a systemic approach to program assessment aligned with disciplines/qualifications.
> Assessment design considerations should span a whole program … rather than be applied solely
> at individual task or unit levels."*

This is a TEQSA-commissioned document explicitly recommending program-level, not task-level,
assessment design as the response to generative AI — which is DFVA's unit of analysis. It does
not validate DFVA's specific items, but it removes any doubt that scoring at the program level
is a category error; the sector's own assessment-reform authors are recommending exactly that
shift. **Proposition 3** ("the process of learning") is the direct precedent for the honest
scope note in §7 below: it identifies "critical thinking, judgement, decision-making … and
reflection on the process" as "components of the process that AI is less able to simulate" —
precisely the inferential leap from handbook text to graduate capability that §4 names as
DFVA's unstated warrant.

**Enacting assessment reform in a time of artificial intelligence** (Lodge, Bearman, Dawson,
Gniel, Harper, Liu, McLean & Ucnik, 2025) — the middle instalment of the TEQSA trilogy, sitting
between the 2023 discussion paper and the June 2026 adaptive-capabilities report. It develops
Proposition 2 above into a full comparison of three institutional pathways for assuring learning
at program level, and names **"programmatic assessment"** (the aggregation-of-evidence-over-time
approach from medical education, e.g. van der Vleuten & Schuwirth) as the working exemplar —
itself a further Tier 2 candidate worth a citation as a methodology model, not for content.

Two passages bear directly on DFVA. Its **Critical Question 1** is the institutional-assurance
mirror of the extrapolation inference isolated in §6:

> *"What mechanisms ensure that unit-level assessments collectively provide evidence that each
> student has met program-level outcomes (as required by the Threshold Standards)?"*

That is the same question DFVA needs to ask of itself, one level up: what mechanism ensures that
a scored handbook — itself an aggregation of unit-level learning outcomes — collectively provides
evidence that *graduates* (not just the documented curriculum) hold the capability being scored.
The paper does not answer this for its own case either; it poses it as an open, ongoing
institutional responsibility, which is the honest framing DFVA's own methodology note should
adopt rather than treating the handbook-to-capability link as settled. **Critical Question 5**
goes further and names the validation burden explicitly: *"How do you know your approach is
genuinely assuring that learning outcomes are being achieved? … What validation processes are
used to verify that supposedly secure assessments are not being routinely compromised?"* — the
same question Woods, Lyons et al. (2026) put to self-constructed instruments generally, now
posed by TEQSA's own authors about assurance-of-learning design specifically.

**DigComp 3.0** (European Digital Competence Framework, JRC144121, the version the Australian
Digital Capability Framework is built on) confirms the five areas cited above are exact, not
paraphrased: *Information search, evaluation and management*; *Communication and collaboration*;
*Digital content creation*; *Safety and wellbeing*; *Problem solving*. Each carries an AI-inflected
competence variant (marked `[AI-I]` throughout the 2024 revision) — e.g. CS1.1.05 *"purposefully
explore new digital search tools and strategies"* — which is the right grain for a C4 anchor:
observable use, not self-reported comfort.

**Jobs and Skills Australia** — two documents, not one. The *National Skills Taxonomy update:
building a system that puts people and skills first* (30 October 2025) sets the "skills-first"
policy direction and a new national definition of *skill* as "a dynamic, purpose-driven human
ability shaped by learning, experience and context" (developed with ACER) — the vocabulary a
v4 crosswalk should borrow, since it is about to become the national standard. JSA's separate
2025 finding that gen AI is "augmenting, not replacing, work" and lifting demand for digital
literacy and human skills is the labour-market complement.

The **Higher Education Standards Framework (Threshold Standards) 2021** — 1.1 graduate
attributes, 1.4 evidence of learning outcomes, 5.2 assessment integrity — is what all of the
above is explicitly operationalising, and is the regulatory anchor a v4 methodology note should
cite directly.

### Tier 2 — psychometrically developed constructs, with instruments

- **Adaptive expertise** (Hatano & Inagaki 1986; Schwartz, Bransford & Sears on *preparation
  for future learning*). The 2025 scoping review in *Advances in Health Sciences Education*
  ([PMC12572040](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12572040/)) maps **19 instruments in
  6 types** across 13 themes — six of adaptive *expertise* (epistemology, innovative skills,
  domain-specific skills, flexibility, metacognition, goals and beliefs) and seven of adaptive
  *performance*. Strong-evidence instruments: Job Adaptability Inventory (Pulakos et al.),
  Charbonnier-Voirin Adaptive Performance Scale, Bohle Carbonell Adaptive Expertise Inventory,
  van Dam Adaptability Scale. **Its headline caveat is also DFVA's problem**: *"a unified
  definition and operationalisation of the concepts is lacking"*, and the instruments are
  dominated by self-report.
- **Master Adaptive Learner** (Cutrer et al.) — planning → learning → assessing → adjusting,
  with a validated instrument (*Medical Science Educator* 2021; revised instrument, *Medical
  Teacher* 2026). Health professions education, i.e. Lyons's own field and vocabulary.
- **Evaluative judgement** (Tai, Ajjawi, Boud, Dawson & Panadero, 2018, *Higher Education*) —
  *"the capability to make decisions about the quality of work of oneself and others."* The
  linchpin of TEQSA's hybrid metacognition, and the single most directly assessable of the
  adaptive capabilities from curriculum documents.
- **Self-regulated learning** — Lodge et al. (2023) state it plainly: *"the foundational skill
  for the AI era is self-regulated learning."*
- **NRC (2012), *Education for Life and Work*** (Pellegrino & Hilton, National Academies) —
  cognitive / intrapersonal / interpersonal competency clusters, with *deeper learning* defined
  as the process producing **transferable** knowledge. A consensus study, and the most
  defensible available taxonomy for the transfer construct.
- **AAC&U VALUE rubrics** — the closest existing thing to a *rubric* with published reliability
  evidence, from two AAC&U sources: the original multi-institution inter-rater pilot reported in
  *Peer Review* (Fall 2011/Winter 2012, *"Assessing Liberal Education Outcomes Using VALUE
  Rubrics"*) and a later national rater-effects study (2018–19 scoring cycle) covering **6,610
  artefacts scored by 221 raters**, with only 8% of scorers flagged for any common rater effect.
  Worth adopting as a *model of practice* — levels written as observable performance, a
  calibration protocol, published rater-effect analysis — even where the content does not
  transfer.
- **AI literacy instruments** — Long & Magerko (2020), 17 competencies, the field's definitional
  reference; UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Students (2024); MAILS (Carolus et al., 2023),
  a validated self-report scale; AICOS (2025), an *objective* rather than self-report measure.
  Use these to widen D5 — but subordinate them to TEQSA's warning about tool-level durability.

### Tier 3 — labour-side task frameworks (these belong on Panel A, not Panel C)

Felten, Raj & Seamans AIOE (already the Panel A basis); **Frey & Osborne's three engineering
bottlenecks** — perception & manipulation, creative intelligence, social intelligence — which
is an empirically derived durability taxonomy in its own right; **Brynjolfsson, Mitchell &
Rock's Suitability for Machine Learning rubric**, 21 items on a 5-point scale applied to 2,059
O*NET detailed work activities; Autor, Levy & Murnane (2003); Acemoglu & Restrepo (2019);
Dell'Acqua et al. (2023/2025) on the jagged frontier.

Two uses. First, as a **cross-check on coverage**: of Frey & Osborne's three bottlenecks, Panel
C addresses creative intelligence weakly (via D7), social intelligence not at all, and
perception/manipulation not at all. Second, the SML rubric is **the methodological template
worth copying** — short declarative statements rated for agreement against a described unit of
work, rather than prose anchors requiring a holistic judgement.

### Tier 4 — domain competency frameworks (the missing upstream leg)

CEPH's 22 MPH foundational competencies across 8 themes; WHO-ASPHER (10 sections, with a
validated self-assessment scale); the Council on Linkages core competencies; and — already
identified in the collaborator brief — AMIA's core competencies and the AHICF. These do not
replace Panel C. They supply the crosswalk that converts a DFVA score from *"a rubric we
invented"* into *"a score expressed in the vocabulary accreditors already use"*.

## 4. The gap the review actually found

**There is no published curriculum-document-level durability rubric.** Every validated
instrument in Tier 2 measures a *person* — by self-report, supervisor rating, or scenario
performance. DFVA scores a *program*, from its public handbook, without touching a student.

That has three consequences, and they should be stated in this order:

1. **There is no instrument to adopt wholesale.** Any claim that DFVA is "based on a validated
   instrument" would be false, and would be caught.
2. **There is an authoritative construct to adopt** — TEQSA's adaptive capabilities — and
   validated item *content* to adapt from Tier 2.
3. **The unit-of-analysis shift is the novel contribution, and the thing most needing a
   validity argument.** DFVA's inference chain is: handbook text → curriculum intent →
   graduate capability. It *observes* the first and *claims about* the third. The warrant for
   intent → capability is constructive alignment (Biggs), which is an assumption the instrument
   currently makes silently. Naming it is not a weakness; leaving it unnamed is.

This is the paper. It is also why the co-authorship ask in the collaborator brief lands: the
gap is a learning-sciences measurement problem, not a data-engineering one.

## 5. Recommended Panel C v4

> **Superseded in one respect** by the formal
> [Panel C v4 recommendation](dfva-panelc-v4-recommendation.md): C1 (deep disciplinary
> knowledge) is resolved there as a **gate**, not a scored item — which is where TEQSA's own
> architecture places it (foundation under the four capabilities), and which preserves the
> 5-item /15 structure and the entire v3.1 enumeration machinery. The sketch below is retained
> as the working-out.

Six items, anchored on the TEQSA framework, each carrying the v3.1 item it replaces so the
migration is auditable.

| v4 item | Anchored on | Absorbs / restores |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **C1 · Deep disciplinary knowledge** | TEQSA foundation ("epistemic foundation for authentic judgement"); NRC transferable knowledge | D3 + the D6 gate — moved *under* adaptiveness as its precondition, not scored as adaptiveness itself (per §2.1 defect 3) |
| **C2 · Distributed cognition** | TEQSA #2; Deming (2017) social skills; Frey & Osborne social intelligence | D2 (partial) + **D8 restored** |
| **C3 · Hybrid metacognition & evaluative judgement** | TEQSA #3; Tai et al. (2018); SRL literature | **New.** The largest single gap in the current instrument |
| **C4 · Digital & AI literacy, including governance** | TEQSA #1; DigComp/ADCF; Long & Magerko; UNESCO 2024 | D5, re-anchored above tool level and widened to governance |
| **C5 · Life-long learning & transfer** | TEQSA #4; NRC deeper learning; adaptive expertise | **D9 restored**, re-anchored on *transfer* rather than on review recency — which is what made it unscoreable from handbooks (r = 0.06) |
| **C6 · Inquiry & evidence generation** | Boyer; Brew; Frey & Osborne creative intelligence | D7, retained largely intact |
| ~~B · Irreplaceability~~ | — | **Retired.** A verdict over other items; its variance is halo (§2.1 defect 4). The irreplaceability claim belongs in the *position* (exposure × adaptiveness), which is already what it says |

Two design rules to carry into the anchor writing:

- **Anchors state observable curriculum evidence, not capability claims.** "Assessment requires
  students to justify when they relied on or overrode an AI system" is checkable in a handbook;
  "graduates exercise evaluative judgement" is not. The SML rubric's declarative-statement form
  is the model.
- **Level 3 must be reachable *and* evidenced.** 53 of 170 scored items (31%) currently sit at
  the ceiling, which drives the −0.075 asymmetric perturbation drift documented in v3.1 §3.4.
  Some of that is anchors that top out too easily.

### 5.1 The honest cost

This is a **new instrument, not a patch**. Adaptiveness scores would not be comparable to
v3.1; the reference medians (exposure 90.9, adaptiveness 10) would re-base; every one of the
38 placed programs could move quadrant. That is precisely the kind of silent re-positioning
the v3.1 extension-cohort protocol forbids — so it must be a deliberate, published v4 cycle
with a migration table, on the pattern of the v1 → v2 transition, not an in-place edit.

### 5.2 Sequencing — the one decision that is time-sensitive

**Do not run the IRR study on v3.1 Panel C.** The protocol as drafted validates the five-item
instrument. If v4 replaces that instrument, the study certifies something already retired, and
the reliability evidence does not transfer to the new items. Either freeze Panel C and run IRR
as designed, or re-anchor first and run IRR on v4 — but the two decisions are coupled, and
running them in the wrong order wastes the single most expensive piece of evidence in the plan.
Given that the rubric revision is now the live question, re-anchoring first is the better bet.

## 6. The validity argument this sets up

Two complementary frameworks, both invoked in the higher-education rubric literature reviewed
above (§3.2), and both worth using rather than picking one.

**Messick's (1989) five aspects** — a checklist of the evidence types a full validity case
needs:

| Aspect | Evidence to produce | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Content** | Delphi panel + content validity index over the v4 items; crosswalk to CEPH/AMIA/AHICF | Not started — the natural first ask, and the natural co-authorship |
| **Internal structure** | Do C1–C6 hold as distinct factors or collapse? Are band cuts defensible? | Not started; item–total data exists from v1 |
| **Response process** | Think-aloud comparison of LLM rater vs human raters on the same handbook | Not started |
| **Relations to other variables** | Panel A exposure, QILT outcomes, JIR destinations | Partially available in-repo |
| **Consequences** | Student-facing harm of band labels on live degrees | Open; flagged in the collaborator brief §5.5 |

**Kane's (2013) argument-based approach** — read in full text for this review, not summary —
is not a fifth aspect but a different organising move: state the **interpretation/use
argument (IUA)** as a chain of inferences, each resting on a **warrant** that needs **backing**,
and look hardest for evidence *against* the weakest link, not evidence confirming the strongest
one. Mapped onto DFVA's actual inference chain (§4):

| Kane inference | DFVA's version | The warrant, stated plainly | Backing currently held |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Scoring** | Handbook text → item score (0–3) | An LLM (or human) rater applying the anchor consistently measures what the anchor describes | None published — this is exactly Woods, Lyons et al.'s (2026) critique |
| **Generalization** | One handbook snapshot → the program's typical state | The scored handbook page is representative, not a favourable outlier | Untested |
| **Extrapolation** | Curriculum intent (as scored) → graduate capability | Constructive alignment: what a curriculum documents as intended, assessed learning actually produces in graduates | **The weakest and least examined link** — never stated as an assumption anywhere in the v1–v3.1 documents, let alone evidenced |
| **Implication / decision** | Position on the matrix → "pivot required" / "resilient" | A curriculum-adaptiveness score together with destination exposure warrants an action-relevant verdict about a real, currently-enrolled cohort | Flagged as open in the collaborator brief §5.5 (the ethics of the band labels) |

Kane's discipline is to spend scrutiny where the chain is weakest, and by that test the
**extrapolation link is the priority**, not content validity. Content validity (which items
belong) is necessary and is the cheaper, more visible ask — it is where a co-authored study
would naturally start, and it is the step with a UoM expert panel already identifiable, since
four of the TEQSA framework's authors are in-house. But it does not, by itself, warrant the
step Kane's framework isolates as the actual site of risk: that a documented curriculum
intention predicts what a graduate can do. That gap should be named in the v4 methodology note
as an explicit, unresolved assumption — not left implicit as it is today.

---

## 7. A note on the sections she set aside

Better anchors fix the score. They do not, on their own, fix §5 Curriculum Implications and
§6 Evidence Confidence — because the issue there is not rubric quality but **unmarked change
of epistemic status**. Part B §1–§4 report what was observed. §5 argues from it. The report
does not currently signal where that switch happens, so a reader who trusts §1–§4 has no
cue that §5 is a different kind of claim. That is a display fix, and it is independent of
the v4 work.
