### Overview
Actuarial science will prepare you for a career as an actuary. To provide professional actuarial advice in Australia, and in most other developed countries, you must become a fellow of the local actuarial profession. This involves developing basic technical skills, learning how to apply these skills in a general setting, developing deep specialist knowledge and completing a professional course following several years' relevant work experience. You will have the opportunity to gain industry-based experience as part of the specialisations in this course: Actuarial studies and Actuarial analytics. You will be able to seek employment in international finance and business fields dealing with life, general or health insurance, superannuation, risk management and financial consulting. Many actuaries and actuarial graduates work on investment operations for asset managers, brokers and banks. There are a range of government-related jobs in regulatory control, workers' compensation, education and others. Some actuaries work with large companies on long-term strategic planning.

### Structure
The Bachelor of Actuarial Science is a specialist course, structured in four parts: Part A. Core studies, Part B. Discipline studies, Part C. Specialist studies and Part D. Elective studies.

Part A. Core studies

These units will provide you with a comprehensive study of economics and econometrics disciplines and the impact they have on multi-discipline decision-making in organisations. The units consider the impact on the business, professional and public policy communities.

Part B. Discipline studies

These units will develop your capacity as a critical and creative professional who is able to apply your knowledge of actuarial science to provide discipline-based solutions to commerce. Units contained within the discipline may meet the requirements for professional accreditation bodies.

Part C. Specialist studies

These units let you focus more deeply on advanced areas of actuarial science. They develop your expertise in applying specialised knowledge and approaches to complex commerce problems, preparing you for professional practice or further study. Units contained within this component may meet the requirements for professional accreditation bodies. In this part you can select your area of interest, either actuarial studies or actuarial analytics. You will also consolidate your knowledge and skills acquired through the completion of a capstone unit as part of your specialist studies.

Part D. Elective studies

These studies will enable you to further develop your knowledge of actuarial science, or commerce more broadly, or to select any units from across the University in which you are eligible to enrol, including to complete a major or minor from another course. 

Masters Accelerated Pathway

Students who are progressing through Parts A, B, and C at a standard required for Actuaries Institute exemptions can use 24 credit points of the 48 credit points to complete four Level 5 units selected from B6060 Stage 1 Part D Application Studies, Additional actuarial studies units as an accelerated pathway into the Master of Actuarial Studies, subject to Faculty approval. Students who then successfully complete the Bachelor of Actuarial Science to the required standard will be eligible for admission into the Master of Actuarial Studies at entry level 3.

## ECC1000 - Principles of microeconomics
Faculty: Department of Economics | School: Faculty of Business and Economics | Credit points: 6

### Overview
This unit introduces the core principles of microeconomics and how they explain decision-making by individuals, firms and governments. You will learn key tools such as supply, demand and market equilibrium, and apply them to everyday economic situations. The unit then examines consumer behaviour, firm production and cost decisions, and how different market structures shape prices, output and strategic choices. You will also be introduced to more advanced topics, including consumer choice theory, behavioural economics and game theory. Throughout the unit, you will use economic reasoning and graphical analysis to interpret real-world issues—such as policy design, inequality and global market differences—developing skills for evidence-based decision-making in business and economics.

### Learning Outcomes
1. FIRST SEMESTER: understand the 'economic way of thinking' about a broad range of individual, business and government choices and decisions SECOND SEMESTER: apply economic concepts to analyse how individuals, firms and governments make economic decisions in competitive and non-competitive market contexts
2. FIRST SEMESTER: apply economic principles to explain and analyse economic institutions, economic decisions and performance SECOND SEMESTER: use graphical and quantitative tools to evaluate how markets allocate resources and how prices adjust in response to economic shocks and policy interventions
3. FIRST SEMESTER: recognise regulatory and political environments and how they impact on business. SECOND SEMESTER: communicate economic insights clearly and professionally, using appropriate economic concepts and evidence in diverse real-world contexts
4. SECOND SEMESTER: evaluate the equity, efficiency, ethical and societal implications of economic decisions in real-world contexts
5. SECOND SEMESTER: contribute problem-solving ideas in a team setting.

## ETC1000 - Business and economic statistics
Faculty: Department of Econometrics and Business Statistics | School: Faculty of Business and Economics | Credit points: 6

### Overview
In this unit, you will learn how to analyse and interpret data to support evidence-based decision-making in business and economics. You will explore methods for summarising data, including tables, charts, and measures of central tendency and dispersion. Key topics include standardisation techniques, regression analysis, and the use of probability and sampling to understand uncertainty. The unit also covers statistical inference, including confidence intervals and hypothesis testing. Throughout, you’ll apply these concepts to real-world scenarios, developing practical skills in data analysis.

2. FIRST SEMESTER: interpret business and economic data using tables, charts and descriptive statistics techniques, applying standardisation techniques where appropriate SECOND SEMESTER: analyse business and economic data using quantitative methods to inform evidence-based decision-making
3. FIRST SEMESTER: make inference on single and multiple population means, difference between means and the slope for business and economic decision-making SECOND SEMESTER: analyse multi-variable data to draw conclusions for business and economic decision-making
4. FIRST SEMESTER: describe the concept of a sampling distribution, estimators and their properties SECOND SEMESTER: apply probability concepts to model uncertainty and assess risk in business and economic contexts
5. FIRST SEMESTER: interpret and evaluate relationships between variables for business and economic decision-making using simple and multiple linear regression SECOND SEMESTER: communicate statistical findings using appropriate formats, collaborating in teams to present evidence-based insights for business audiences

## ETC2410 - Introductory econometrics
Faculty: Department of Econometrics and Business Statistics | School: Faculty of Business and Economics | Credit points: 6

### Overview
This unit introduces you to the empirical analysis of relationships between economic variables. The approach is based on linear regression theory, and emphasises 'hands on' data analysis. Topics studied will include properties of least squares estimators, hypothesis testing, the choice of appropriate functional form, the use of dummy variables, issues around modelling survey data and the problems of serial correlation, heteroscedasticity and multicollinearity.

### Learning Outcomes
1. identify the presence of heteroscedasticity, adjust OLS standard errors and perform feasible GLS in regression models
2. interpret, evaluate and apply inferential methods to multiple linear regression
3. understand issues related to modelling with time-series data.
4. understand and derive the properties of ordinary least squares in summation and matrix notation
5. understand the use and implications of data scaling, functional form and dummy variables in regression modelling
