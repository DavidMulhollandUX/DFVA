## Description
Students commencing prior to 2024 should refer to the program rules for the year in which they started.

The Bachelor of Criminology & Criminal Justice (BCCJ) is a three-year full-time, or six-year part-time equivalent, undergraduate degree that offers a critical, interdisciplinary exploration of crime, justice, and social change. Grounded in research-led teaching and real-world engagement, the BCCJ challenges students to think beyond traditional approaches to criminology and to examine how justice systems impact individuals and communities.

The program brings together expertise from criminology, sociology, law, public health, youth work, and policy studies. Students engage with diverse perspectives, including from those with lived experience of the criminal justice system, and explore topics such as criminalisation, policing, incarceration, alternative justice systems, and the impacts of colonisation and systemic inequality.

The BCCJ features two integrated core curricula: the Criminology & Criminal Justice Core and the Social Science Core. This structure allows students to develop both a deep understanding of criminological theory and practice, and strong applied skills in social research and policy analysis. Students can choose to further build their knowledge and skills through the range of work-integrated learning and internship opportunities available in the program or by undertaking one of the four double degree options. The versatility of the program prepares graduates for impactful careers across justice, policy, advocacy, and community sectors.

## Career Opportunities & Employment Areas
You will graduate with interdisciplinary skills applicable to a diverse range of career opportunities in government agencies, private corporations and the education sector. You'll learn to think expansively about issues and develop skills such as ethical and social responsibility, policy analysis and research methods that are critical to success across many fields.

As a criminologist, you can apply your skills to make a difference in crime prevention, law enforcement, corrective services, terrorism, cybercrime, policy design and more.
The Bachelor of Criminology & Criminal Justice integrates career readiness training through our Criminology Capstone course, Criminology in Practice, alongside mentoring programs, job-seeking modules, and professional career support opportunities. In this course, you'll draw together key concepts, themes and debates in criminology and apply them to current issues. You'll graduate with a portfolio of work that touches on eight current themes within criminology.

Subject: Disability in the Criminal Justice System (CRIM2032)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: This course explores current theoretical, policy and practice-oriented debates about how and why people with disability are overrepresented in all stages of criminal justice systems across western societies. The course will put these current debates in context by exploring interdisciplinary materials and approaches - including critical criminology, penal abolition, decoloniality, disability justice and critical disability studies - that provide important insights about the role of historical, social- structural and cultural factors, as well as forms of state violence, abuse and neglect in the increasing criminalisation and imprisonment of people with disability. We will hear from criminalised people with disability, as well as leading advocates and practitioners working in the field. A key aim of the course is to develop a nuanced understanding of how disability as a key analytic category and identity (alongside class, race and gender/sexuality) is central to developing a deeper understanding of the functions, purpose and consequences of criminal justice systems. A related aim is to explore how this knowledge contributes to the development of alternatives to criminal justice that are rooted in the principles of social, restorative and transformative justice.
Learning Outcomes:
- Demonstrate creativity and innovation through consideration of alternatives to criminalisation and punishment
- Identify and explain the role of historical, social-structural and cultural factors, as well forms of state violence, abuse and neglect in the current overrepresentation of people with disability in criminal justice systems
- Demonstrate in-depth understanding of the complex intersections between disability and criminal justice systems
- Articulate how a critical and reflexive interdisciplinary theoretical analysis of disability assists in understanding the purpose, functions and consequences of criminal justice systems
