Overview
Award title	Master of Counselling
Year & campus	2026 — University of Melbourne Online
CRICOS code	This course is available to domestic students only, and is not available to international students.
Fees information	Subject EFTSL, level, discipline and census date
Study level & type	Graduate Coursework
AQF level	9
Credit points	150 credit points
Duration	18 months full-time or 36 months part-time

This course is delivered across six online terms per year whereby students can enrol in up to one 12.5 credit point subject per online teaching term. This equates to a full-time study load of 0.75 EFTSL (equivalent full-time student load) per year. Please note, the course duration listed is based on a study load of 1 EFTSL – duration will vary for students taking a lesser study load and/or with advanced standing.

The Master of Counselling is a mixed-mode course. The majority of subjects will be delivered online. However, students are required to attend and complete a five-day in-person residential subject at the University of Melbourne’s Parkville campus and also complete 200-hours of supervised in-person placement.

The Master of Counselling provides advanced training in evidence-informed, culturally responsive counselling practice. The course prepares students for ethical, reflective, and effective practice in diverse settings across the health, education, community, and private sectors.

Students will develop advanced skills in assessment, case formulation, intervention, and therapeutic engagement through interactive learning, supervised practice, and in-depth self-reflection. The course fosters critical thinking, professional identity formation, and trauma-informed, socially just approaches to counselling. In the Masters experience, students will study advanced methods for counselling including drawing on a range of frameworks and approaches.

All students must complete a compulsory, in-person 200-hour supervised placement under the guidance of a qualified clinical supervisor. Placement may be online (video/phone) or conducted at the premises of a host organization. All efforts are made to ensure that placement allocations are within 1.5 hours travel time (one way), however this cannot be guaranteed. In the case of regional and remote students, longer travel or relocation may be required. Students must complete an Australian Police Check and relevant state-based screening (e.g. Working With Children or Working With Vulnerable People Checks) to undertake placement.

If, since turning 16, you have lived in another country for 12 continuous months or more, or you currently hold citizenship or permanent residency in another country (including dual citizenship), you must provide one of the following:

A completed "Overseas Police Record Declaration"
Nation wide check of criminal history by relevant national authority in applicable country/ies
International Criminal History Check by approved supplier

A five-day in-person intensive workshop forms part of the curriculum, held at the University of Melbourne’s Parkville campus, with additional locations considered based on demand.

A compulsory five-day in-person residential subject forms part of the curriculum. Students are required to attend the University of Melbourne’s Parkville campus, in person for the full five days. Additional locations considered based on demand.

Students are responsible for all costs associated with attending the residential, including travel, accommodation, meals and any other incidental expenses. Further information about the Residential Experience is available on the subject page in the Student Handbook.

In the final phase of the program, students will complete a capstone experience focused on a practice project or applied research, enabling them to apply their learning to practice innovation, policy development, or research inquiry. This final component supports students to build a strong foundation for leadership, advocacy, or further academic study.

Intended learning outcomes

On completion of this course, graduates should be able to:

Integrate advanced theoretical knowledge of counselling frameworks to support diverse individuals and communities;
Employ culturally responsive and ethically sound practices, with a demonstrated commitment to equity, social justice, and strengths-based engagement;
Apply and adapt advanced interpersonal and communication skills to build therapeutic relationships and facilitate collaboration in diverse contexts;
Critically evaluate current mental health and healthcare research and apply insights to complex real-world counselling scenarios;
Reflect on their professional identity and practice with insight, adaptability, and a commitment to ongoing professional learning and supervision;
Design, implement, and evaluate counselling interventions that are evidence-informed, contextually relevant, and responsive to emerging client and sector needs;
Evaluate clinical decision making and innovative approaches to counselling practice, policy, or systems.
Generic skills
communicate in ways relevant to both academic and practice contexts;
link theory to practice;
work collaboratively;
apply critical thinking, analytic and problem-solving skills to unfamiliar challenges;
actively participate in self-reflection as it relates to performance in the workplace.
Graduate attributes

Academic distinction:

The Melbourne Experience prepares well-rounded graduates who are academically outstanding and have research and reasoning skills that equip them to be influential citizens with high leadership potential.

Graduates of the Master of Counselling should:

have comprehensive knowledge and understanding of counselling theories and frameworks
have a critical understanding of current research-based knowledge to response to complex client needs in an evolving sector;
be able to integrate theoretical and research-based knowledge to respond to complex client needs in an evolving sector;
be effective oral and written communicators; and
be adept lifelong learners who are committed to ongoing professional growth.

Active citizenship:

The Melbourne Experience prepares graduates who are socially responsible, engage with national global issues, and are attuned to social and cultural diversity.

Graduates of the Master of Counselling should:

understand, and respect, the ethical, cultural, and social justice principles that underpin professional counselling practice;
be prepared to contribute to professional counselling practice through counselling policy and systems;
be able to work collaboratively with people from diverse linguistic and cultural backgrounds;
understand and respect Indigenous knowledge, culture, and values; and
have the potential to be leaders in their professions and communities.

Integrity and self-awareness:

The Melbourne Experience prepares graduates who are practically grounded, have high levels of self-awareness, and value their personal integrity and well-being.

Graduates of the Master of Counselling should:

be self-aware and reflective, willing to adapt to client needs;
accept and give constructive feedback;
be motivated, self-directed, and well-organised;
have empathy and concern for the welfare of others;
be enthusiastic, confident in their knowledge, yet flexible and aware of their limitations; and
be able to manage their own well-being.

Please note: this subject is delivered wholly online and only open to students enrolled in the mixed mode courses Master of Counselling, Graduate Diploma in Counselling and the wholly online Graduate Certificate in Counselling. Subjects in this course are delivered in an online accelerated learning model and therefore, students typically enrol in one 12.5 credit point subject per online teaching term.

This subject introduces foundational and contemporary counselling theories that underpin ethical and effective practice. It explores a range of contemporary frameworks, including psychodynamic, person-centred, cognitive behavioural, and narrative approaches. Students will examine the philosophical assumptions, core concepts, and techniques associated with each model, developing the capacity to critically compare and apply them to diverse counselling contexts. These frameworks provide a strong theoretical foundation for those pursuing future professional counselling practice, supervision, or research, and serve as a basis for integrating culturally responsive and evidence-informed approaches.

Intended learning outcomes

On completion of this subject, students should be able to:

Identify a range of theories and frameworks that inform current counselling practice;
Analyse and compare core counselling theories and frameworks;
Justify application of diverse frameworks to case scenarios using theory and research;
Evaluate the complexities of counselling in a multicultural context.
Generic skills

On completion of this subject, students should be able to:

Articulate and adapt theoretical knowledge to practical scenarios​;
Communicate in ways relevant to both academic and practice contexts;
Communicate effectively in oral and digital forms;
Work in collaboration with others.

In this practice-based subject, students focus on developing interpersonal, reflective, and communication skills required for counselling. Through experiential learning, including role-plays, peer feedback, and structured simulations, students will build confidence in applying foundational micro-skills such as active listening, empathy, summarising, and questioning. Students will also begin to explore their own values, responses, and presence in the counselling process. Guided by feedback from peers and facilitators, students will have opportunities to reflect on their communication style and areas for growth. This subject prepares students for future residential and placement by building their capacity to form safe, ethical, and collaborative therapeutic relationships.

Intended learning outcomes

Identify and describe the role of micro-skills in interpersonal communication;
Describe how their self-knowledge and self-awareness can inform client interactions;
Demonstrate core communication and counselling skills;
Critically self-reflect on their interactions with others;
Constructively appraise their practice and the practice of others.
Generic skills
Give and receive constructive feedback;
Integrate personal reflection in the communication process;
Communicate effectively in oral and digital forms;
Link theory to practice;
Work in collaboration with others.

Please note: this subject is delivered wholly online and only open to students enrolled in the mixed mode courses Master of Counselling, Graduate Diploma in Counselling and the wholly online Graduate Certificate in Counselling. Subjects in this course are delivered in an online accelerated learning model and therefore, students typically enrol in one 12.5 credit point subject per online teaching term.

This subject introduces students to the ethical, legal, and professional foundations of contemporary counselling practice. Students examine national and international codes of ethics, statutory obligations, and regulatory frameworks that guide decision-making and protect client wellbeing. Through case studies and discussion, students consider confidentiality, record-keeping, informed consent, and boundary management, and evaluate how culture, power, and intersectionality shape ethical responses. Practical activities encourage students to apply ethical-reasoning models to complex scenarios and to reflect on their emerging professional identity and responsibility for self-care and supervision. The unit equips students to uphold culturally competent, accountable practice in placement and beyond.

Intended learning outcomes

On completion of this subject, students should be able to:

Identify core professional responsibilities in counselling and the theories and frameworks that inform supervision relationships;
Describe the influence of codes of ethics, legal principles, and personal and professional values on counselling practice and ethical decision-making processes;
Critically assess ethical issues and dilemmas and utilise steps in the ethical decision-making process;
Apply the ethical and legal responsibilities of counsellors to complex case scenarios;
Design a supervision and self-care plan and critically reflect on the integration of theory and self-awareness.
Generic skills
Communicate in ways relevant to both academic and practice contexts;
Link theory to practice;
Work collaboratively;
Apply critical thinking, analytical and problem-solving skills to unfamiliar challenges.

In this subject, students develop skills in comprehensive client assessment and psychosocial formulation. Design effective intervention strategies by critically applying practice theories to relevant client circumstances and contexts.

Please note: this subject is delivered wholly online and only open to students enrolled in the mixed-mode courses Master of Counselling and Graduate Diploma in Counselling. Subjects in this course are delivered in an online accelerated learning model and therefore, students typically enrol in one 12.5 credit point subject per online teaching term.

In this subject, students will develop the knowledge and skills required to respond safely, ethically, and effectively to trauma across life stages and settings. Students will explore how trauma—including complex trauma and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)—impacts the brain, body, and behaviour, and examine its implications for therapeutic work. The subject critically examines contemporary trauma theories, including psychodynamic, narrative, and anti-oppressive approaches and explores their application to working with individuals, families and communities in counselling practice settings. Through case studies, contemporary research, and applied learning, students will learn to integrate trauma-informed principles into assessment and planning. The subject also focuses on the risks of vicarious trauma and burnout, equipping students to build sustainable self-care strategies. This subject prepares students to work with individuals and communities affected by trauma in diverse counselling contexts.

Intended learning outcomes

On completion of this subject, students should be able to:

Describe contemporary theories of traumatic stress responses and articulate the skills for working with individuals and families in trauma-informed ways;
Critically evaluate the impact of complex trauma, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and systemic oppression on client wellbeing and treatment approaches;
Critically assess and reflect on the risks of vicarious trauma, compassion fatigue, and burnout in counselling contexts;
Apply trauma-informed principles and frameworks to therapeutic assessment, case conceptualisation, and intervention planning;
Develop and communicate a sustainable self-care and supervision plan that supports ethical and reflective trauma-informed practice.
Generic skills
Communicate effectively, in oral, written and digital forms;
Apply theoretical thinking and awareness  across domains;
Link theory to practice;
Work collaboratively.

Please note: this subject is delivered wholly online and only open to students enrolled in the mixed mode courses Master of Counselling and Graduate Diploma in Counselling. Subjects in this course are delivered in an online accelerated learning model and therefore, students typically enrol in one 12.5 credit point subject per online teaching term.

Understanding mental illness is essential to ethical, person-centred counselling practice. In this subject, students will examine contemporary approaches to mental health and wellbeing through different lenses including lived experience, recovery-oriented practice, and trauma-informed care. Students will explore key diagnostic categories and the impact of stigma, social determinants, and systemic barriers on help-seeking. How mental health system services are organised and the contributions of different mental health professionals and the lived and living experienced workforce is provided. A strong emphasis is placed on communication strategies that enable ethical engagement, mental health literacy, and safe, appropriate referral. Using case-based and experiential learning, students will develop the skills to recognise distress, respond with empathy, and support pathways to culturally safe and inclusive care across diverse populations.​

Intended learning outcomes

On completion of this subject, students should be able to:

Compare and analyse contemporary frameworks of mental illness and wellbeing, including diagnostic, recovery-oriented, and lived experience perspectives;
Evaluate the role of social, cultural, and systemic factors in shaping experiences of mental health, stigma, and recovery;
Reflect critically on personal and professional responses to mental health disclosures, identifying strategies for safe, inclusive, and collaborative practice;
Apply ethical and culturally responsive approaches to recognising, responding to, and referring clients experiencing psychological distress;
Critically evaluate mental health issues, by synthesising and analysing the evidence from a variety of practice perspectives, risk perspectives, and lived experience perspectives and applying them to a range of counselling practice scenarios.
Generic skills
Communicate effectively, in oral, written and digital forms;
Apply theoretical thinking and awareness across domains;
Link theory to practice;
Work collaboratively.

Please note: this subject is delivered in mixed-mode and only open to students enrolled in the Master of Counselling and Graduate Diploma in Counselling. Subjects in this course are delivered in an accelerated learning model and therefore, students typically enrol in one 12.5 credit point subject per online teaching term.

In this hybrid subject, students will blend flexible online study with a five-day residential intensive in Parkville. Building on the first six subjects, students will consolidate their counselling knowledge and micro-skills through supervised practice sessions, immersive virtual simulations, and structured peer feedback. Both online and face-to-face activities emphasise theory–practice integration, ethical decision making, and culturally responsive engagement. After the residential, students will create an evidence-based professional development plan that maps directly to their upcoming placement and final-year capstone. By the end of the subject, students will demonstrate a clearer professional identity, clinical judgement, and a targeted roadmap for growth—ensuring they are fully prepared for counselling placements.

Intended learning outcomes

On completion of this subject, students should be able to:

Describe professional and ethical expectations for supervised counselling placement;
Apply advanced counselling skills in supervised simulations integrating theoretical, ethical and culturally responsive frameworks;
Evaluate and apply supervisor, peer, and client feedback to develop clinical readiness for counselling settings including the placement experience;
Critically reflect on professional identity, supervision experiences, and self-care needs to guide ongoing growth in counselling practice;
Design a personalised professional development plan that aligns with the standards and the requirements of practice placements and future professional counselling work.
Generic skills

On completion of this subject, students should be able to:

Link theory to practice;
Work collaboratively;
Apply critical thinking, analytic and problem-solving skills to unfamiliar challenges;
Actively participate in self-reflection as it relates to performance in the workplace.

This subject explores Indigenous and decolonising approaches to counselling, critically examining how global north models of counselling have often marginalised Indigenous worldviews and healing practices. Grounded in respect for cultural diversity, the subject introduces students to Indigenous knowledge systems, relationality, community-centred healing and the role of story, land and spirituality in wellbeing. The subject examines narrative approaches and the importance of culturally safe, strengths-based and trauma-informed practices. Through critical reflection, case studies and experiential learning, students will develop practical strategies for integrating decolonising principles into their professional counselling practice.

Intended learning outcomes

On completion of this subject, students should be able to:

Evaluate and apply decolonising frameworks and Indigenous models of care to support culturally safe and strengths-based counselling practice;
Critically analyse the historical and contemporary interface between non-Indigenous and Indigenous cultures in Australia and the implications for counselling practice;
Critically reflect on cultural identity, power, and privilege within the context of Indigenous Australians and communities;
Demonstrate cultural humility and awareness of Indigenous world views, values, and relational approaches to healing within counselling contexts;
Evaluate culturally safe practices in individual and systemic contexts.
Generic skills
Engage in respectful discussions;
Link theory to practice;
Work collaboratively;
Communicate with diverse groups.

The Counselling Practice Innovation capstone draws on the theory, knowledge, skills and practice experience gained throughout the Master of Counselling program. Students reflect upon and further explore key learnings to design a practice innovation or service improvement project related to their areas of interest and relevant to their future development as a professional counsellor.
