## Description
The Master of War Studies at UNSW Canberra is designed to give a comprehensive understanding of war as a phenomenon. It takes a global and interdisciplinary approach, allowing the student to explore the causes, course, resolution, and consequences of armed conflict. Students may study various themes: the theory and dimensions of warfare and military strategy; military history and the course of major wars that have shaped our world; special operations and irregular warfare; contemporary and emerging conflicts; and the social, political, ethical, and humanitarian issues of war. The program develops specialised understanding and analytical skills essential for navigating complex geopolitical environments. It is well-suited to professions engaged in conflict analysis, resolution, and strategic planning, including military and defence, intelligence and policy analysis, government agencies and NGOs, as well as teaching, and for anyone interested in developing their understanding in military studies.

## Program Learning Outcomes (CILOs)
On successful completion of this program, students will be able to:

4. Demonstrate high level communication skills, technological literacy, and the ability to form autonomous judgement with clarity and accuracy on war as a phenomenon.
3. Analyse, reflect on, and synthesise, information, concepts, and problems, to demonstrate understanding of different perspectives and evidence-based reasoning.
2. Engage in research and enquiry, using and critically evaluating advanced scholarly sources
1. Demonstrate specialised and integrated understanding the phenomenon of war and debates relevant to the study of armed conflict in global, historical, and contemporary contexts.

## Progression & Academic Rules
Students who do not complete the full Master program requirements may exit the program with a Graduate Certificate in War Studies,  qualification (24 UOC). Of the four courses, students must have completed a minimum of two core courses and two other courses (core or elective) from the 8571 program. For any advanced standing granted on entry into the Masters program, a maximum of 12 UOC advanced standing can be applied to the Graduate Certificate. It is still a requirement that the student complete at least one core course at UNSW.

## Admission Requirements
**Minimum Entry Requirements**:
To gain entry into a Master of War Studies (8571), an applicant must meet one of the following entry requirements:

1.    Completion of a Bachelor degree with honours in the same or a related discipline* from a recognised institution; or

2.    Completion of a Graduate Diploma or Graduate Certificate in the same or a related discipline* from a recognised institution; or

3.    Completion of a Bachelor degree in the same or a related discipline* from a recognised institution; and completion of at least three years relevant full-time professional experience; or

4.    Completion of a Bachelor degree in a non-related discipline; and completion of at least four years relevant full-time professional experience; or

5.    Evidence of other qualifications and professional experience to be assessed as acceptable grounds for admission into the program by the relevant Program Authority. In certain circumstances, students may be required to undertake and successfully complete a relevant non-award course as a condition for admission into the program. This non-award course may also be credited towards the program upon admission.

Subject: Representations of War (ZHSS8105)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: This course will introduce students to key concepts surrounding the ways in which wars are represented, and hence how war comes to be known about in the social and cultural domains. Our key question, then, is how do we know about war? What are the ways that war is reported, analysed, mediated, commemorated, and spectacularised? Such questions about the representation of war have received attention from a range of disciplines because they are central to our understanding of war’s relationship to society. Can wars be waged without public visibility and hence public support? This course will introduce students to some foundational questions about war and its representation. Texts studied may range from literature, art, and commemorative practices that shape the ‘human face of war’, to propaganda, journalism, and social media, and on to broader considerations about war as a cultural event that is always at some level determined by beliefs, perceptions, and memories of war. With its emphasis on critical reading and writing skills, this course is ideally suited to supplement the pleasure and success you can gain from the other offerings in the Masters Programs.
Learning Outcomes:
- Demonstrate a critical understanding of key concepts and debates required for the study of war and its cultural, media and social representations.
- Demonstrate advanced skills in analyzing complex forms of representation, across a range of genres and media.
- Undertake independent research into the relationship between war, culture and representation and effectively communicate research findings.
- Understand the historical, cultural, social, ethical, and political contexts that shape processes of representing war.

Subject: War and Memory (ZHSS8106)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: This course will introduce students to the key concepts from the booming field of memory studies that allow us to explain why debates about the past can be so heated – what is at stake in these discussions? What enables, and indeed compels, us to “remember” conflicts that we did not experience personally? What are the ethical limits of such memorialisation? How do memories change across time? And what is “forgotten” in this process? In pursuit of answers to these and many other questions, we will pair our theoretical readings with a range of materials that memorialise some of the key moments of the 20th and 21st centuries, for example: the First World War; the Holocaust; the Cold War; and the recent conflicts in Iraq and Afghanistan. Materials studied may include novels, memoirs, anthologies of soldiers’ writings, documentaries, feature films and websites.
Learning Outcomes:
- On successful conclusion of this course, students will be able to demonstrate a critical understanding of key concepts and debates from the field of memory studies as they relate to the study of war and memory
- On successful conclusion of this course, students will be able to demonstrate advanced skills in responding to complex forms of representation, across a range of genres and media
- On successful conclusion of this course, students will be able to undertake independent research into the relationship between war, memory and representation
- On successful conclusion of this course, students will be able to analyse the historical, cultural and political contexts that shape processes of remembering and forgetting

Credit Points: 6
Overview: This course examines genocide from the mid-twentieth century to the present. Overview: A common distinction made between special operations forces (SOF) and their general purpose counterparts is that SOF are 'strategic' troops. But is this so? And if so, what does ‘strategic’ mean in this context? This course explores the strategic utility of Special Operations (SO) and the forces that carry them out, and examines the competing and complementary theories of SO. As a core course in the Master of Special Operations and Irregular Warfare, content is designed to give you a comprehensive and integrated understanding of special operations as a military instrument. As an elective in other Master programs, the course will develop your expertise in the study of strategy and security. You will study various strategic theories, including the notion of using SO to induce 'strategic paralysis', often associated with airpower theorists like Giulio Douhet and Billy Mitchell. These are contrasted with attrition-based SO strategies, such as that proposed by James Kiras. Influential 'kinetic' theories of SO, like that of William McRaven, are compared and contrasted with strategies of influence which emphasise the 'indirect approach' of SOF in unconventional warfare. Using this theory, you will analyse the current utility of Special Operations in the context of contemporary major-power competition. Via your assessments, you will be asked to explore a broad range of options, such as bureaucratic control of SO, modernisation requirements of SO, personnel development or selection requirements of SO, or the application of SO theory to other elements of Government (such as policing counter-terrorism requirements).
Learning Outcomes:
- Demonstrate the ability to independently research unorthodox, irregular, and/or asymmetric topics in strategic studies and international relations.
- Demonstrate a clear and integrated understanding of strategic and operational theory relevant to the employment of SOF.
- Effectively communicate, in a focused manner, with scholarly sources and with a well-structured and well-supported argument or counter-argument.
- Demonstrate the ability to interact analytically and respectfully with scholarly debates and the ideas of peers.

Subject: GRIM Threats (Guerrilla, Revolutionary, Insurgent and Militia) and Irregular Warfare (ZHSS8461)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: Conventional Warfare, or warfare by convention, is the dominant Western approach to converting tactical military action into strategic and political ends and is challenged by irregular forms of warfare at the strategic level. Western tactical dominance has encouraged the adoption of irregular approaches to generating strategic effect. This course explores the employment of irregular warfare strategies and the forces that carry them out. In so doing, it implicitly aims to assist a Western force in developing counters to such threats. This course forms a core course for the Masters in Special Operations / Irregular Warfare, and is an elective in the Strategy and Security and War Studies programs.
Learning Outcomes:
- Effectively communicate, in a focused manner, a well-structured and well supported argument or counter-argument.
- Explain unconventional warfare theories, from tactical considerations through strategic effect.
- Demonstrate the skills of independent research on an uncommon topic in strategic studies and international relations.
- Analyse the challenges of complex unconventional threats against conventional militaries.

Subject: Military Game Changers in 21st Century Warfare (ZHSS8467)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: While the nature of war does not change, the character of war unquestionably does. During this course we explore four key disruptive capabilities changing the character of 21st Century warfare: special operations, cyber war, autonomous weapons systems and space operations. After learning the basics of each capability we will look at its impact on operational and tactical levels of the military, as well as discuss implications for international relations and the law and finally, the ethical considerations. The course will require students to hypothesise how these capabilities will combine and interact in potential future conflict.
