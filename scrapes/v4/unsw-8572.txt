## Description
The Master of Strategy and Security is designed to give advanced knowledge of the factors shaping the national, regional, and global strategic and security environments and understanding of the complex policy challenges presented by contemporary and emerging security agendas. It develops the ability to analyse these factors in various ways: strategic planning; managing international and regional relations; the role of international organisations, diplomacy, and law; traditional, human, and cyber security; non-state actors; social, environmental, cultural, and ethical issues; and the challenges of contemporary and emerging conflicts and disruptive technologies. The program is suited to the student seeking to understand statecraft in strategy and security: defence professionals and analysts in foreign affairs, intelligence, government agencies, and NGOs.

## Program Learning Outcomes (CILOs)
On successful completion of this program, students will be able to:

4. Communicate effectively and confidently in written, oral, and technological forms appropriate to academic and policy discourse on security matters.
1. Demonstrate in-depth knowledge and an integrated understanding of global, regional, and national strategic and security environment, and be able to apply this understanding to practical policy problems in a contemporary setting
2. Engage in research and enquiry, using and critically evaluating advanced scholarly sources
3. Analyse, reflect on, and synthesise complex information, demonstrating evidence-based logic and reasoning in the context of understanding strategy and security.

## Progression & Academic Rules
Students who do not complete the full Master program requirements may exit the program with a Graduate Certificate in Strategy and Security, qualification (24 UOC). Of the four courses, students must have completed a minimum of two core courses and two other courses (core or elective) from the 8572 program. For any advanced standing granted on entry into the Masters program, a maximum of 12 UOC advanced standing can be applied to the Graduate Certificate. It is still a requirement that the student complete at least one core course at UNSW.

## Admission Requirements
**Minimum Entry Requirements**:
To gain entry into a Master of Strategy and Security (8572), an applicant must meet one of the following entry requirements:

1.    Completion of a Bachelor degree with honours in the same or a related discipline* from a recognised institution; or

2.    Completion of a Graduate Diploma or Graduate Certificate in the same or a related discipline* from a recognised institution; or

3.    Completion of a Bachelor degree in the same or a related discipline* from a recognised institution; and completion of at least three years relevant full-time professional experience; or

4.    Completion of a Bachelor degree in a non-related discipline; and completion of at least four years relevant full-time professional experience; or

5.    Evidence of other qualifications and professional experience to be assessed as acceptable grounds for admission into the program by the relevant Program Authority. In certain circumstances, students may be required to undertake and successfully complete a relevant non-award course as a condition for admission into the program. This non-award course may also be credited towards the program upon admission.

Subject: Nearest Neighbours: Asia-Pacific Literature, Culture and Communication (ZHSS8103)
Credit Points: 6

- Develop skills in applied research methods, assessing and employing scholarly sources and interdisciplinary approaches to broad strategic issues.
- Demonstrate sound contemporary understanding of a range of Australian geopolitical, social and cultural responses to its Asia Pacific neighbours.
- Further develop advanced skills in communicating evidence-based analysis and effective arguments.

Credit Points: 6
Overview: The course aims to provide students with an understanding of the critical but often overlooked contribution of sea power to history and the development of national security strategies. By studying navies and sea power in the modern world, from the 1890s to the present, concentrating on various thematic issues, students will be challenged to analyse the role that sea power has played in shaping the modern international order and the historical evolution of the concept. - Demonstrate the ability to develop coherent arguments and clearly express them in formal academic writing.
- Demonstrate a broad understanding of the development and use of naval power in the twentieth century.
- Comprehend, and engage with, the key debates in maritime strategy.

Subject: Genocide: Perception and Intervention (ZHSS8210)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: This course examines genocide from the mid-twentieth century to the present. Beginning with the emergence of historical and legal concept in the United Nations Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide, students will explore major case studies, including Armenia, the Holocaust, Cambodia under the Khmer Rouge, Bosnia, Rwanda, and questions of genocide in the contemporary world. - Engage in the ongoing scholarly debate on the definition and understanding of genocide
- Evaluate the difficulties of prevention, intervention and punishment of genocide
- Analyse the main instances or questions and causes of genocide from the beginning of the twentieth century to the present
- Explain and interpret the legal definition of the crime of genocide in the UN Convention

Overview: This course examines peace as a central ordering concept of international relations and introduces a conflict transformation approach to conflict and post-conflict analysis. Beginning with a critical survey of the field, the course develops an understanding of the complex policy challenges associated with peace negotiations and explores different approaches through consideration of protracted conflicts. The field of peace research has developed significantly over recent decades leveraging new technologies for measuring peace. Conflict transformation theory recognises the complexity of conflict dynamics (not having neat beginnings, middles, and endings) and observes that the challenge of ‘managing’ or ‘resolving’ protracted conflict through peace processes have themselves often been the source of deep resentment and justification for further violence. Transforming protracted conflicts requires more than the redistribution of material resources or reframing of positions into resolved outcomes. The approach builds upon a deeper analysis of identity of stakeholders, issues, historical grievances, structural factors, and divergent world views. We will consider the UN concept of peacebuilding and evaluate its effectiveness in addressing the root causes of conflict, promoting justice, and fostering reconciliation among conflicting parties
Learning Outcomes:
- Engage in research and enquiry, using and critically evaluating advanced scholarly sources.
- Demonstrate specialised and integrated understanding of conflict analysis theories and be able to apply this understanding to practical policy problems in a contemporary setting.
- Analyse, reflect on, and synthesise, information, concepts, and problems, to demonstrate understanding of different perspectives and evidence-based reasoning
- Communicate effectively and confidently in written, oral, and technological forms appropriate to academic and policy discourse on conflict transformation and peacebuilding matters.

Subject: Special Operations: Theory and Strategic Utility (ZHSS8459)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: A common distinction made between special operations forces (SOF) and their general purpose counterparts is that SOF are 'strategic' troops. But is this so? And if so, what does ‘strategic’ mean in this context? This course explores the strategic utility of Special Operations (SO) and the forces that carry them out, and examines the competing and complementary theories of SO. As a core course in the Master of Special Operations and Irregular Warfare, content is designed to give you a comprehensive and integrated understanding of special operations as a military instrument. As an elective in other Master programs, the course will develop your expertise in the study of strategy and security. You will study various strategic theories, including the notion of using SO to induce 'strategic paralysis', often associated with airpower theorists like Giulio Douhet and Billy Mitchell. These are contrasted with attrition-based SO strategies, such as that proposed by James Kiras. Influential 'kinetic' theories of SO, like that of William McRaven, are compared and contrasted with strategies of influence which emphasise the 'indirect approach' of SOF in unconventional warfare. Using this theory, you will analyse the current utility of Special Operations in the context of contemporary major-power competition. Via your assessments, you will be asked to explore a broad range of options, such as bureaucratic control of SO, modernisation requirements of SO, personnel development or selection requirements of SO, or the application of SO theory to other elements of Government (such as policing counter-terrorism requirements).
Learning Outcomes:
- Demonstrate the ability to independently research unorthodox, irregular, and/or asymmetric topics in strategic studies and international relations.
- Demonstrate a clear and integrated understanding of strategic and operational theory relevant to the employment of SOF.
- Effectively communicate, in a focused manner, with scholarly sources and with a well-structured and well-supported argument or counter-argument.
- Demonstrate the ability to interact analytically and respectfully with scholarly debates and the ideas of peers.

Subject: Ethics in Special, Complex and Irregular Military Operations (ZHSS8462)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: “Conventional” military operations, in which the uniformed military forces of one state engage with an equivalent force of another state, have become the exception rather than the norm in contemporary warfare. Military forces now more often find themselves pitted against non-state (or hybrid) opponents who avoid symmetric military engagements, preferring to seek shelter among civilian populations while employing a range of asymmetric guerrilla and terrorist tactics in operations located in the 'grey zone' between war and peace. States are making increasing use of special operations forces and other unconventional capabilities (such as cyber/information warfare operations) to carry out 'grey zone' missions, and those same special operations forces find themselves fighting alongside and enabling irregular and non-state forces which employ a mode of warfare that is distinctly unconventional. These realities considerably complicate the existing challenges of conducting operations in a manner consistent with the ethical norms for the use of military force. This course students will give the student detailed knowledge of concepts at the heart of the ethics of war and an understanding of how to apply ethical frameworks in analysing the challenges that arise from military actions in the operational environment of special, complex, and irregular operations.
Learning Outcomes:
- Demonstrate highly developed communication skills at postgraduate level.
- Construct a well-structured argument, demonstrating evidence-based reasoning.
- Research independently and critically evaluate topics in military ethics of complex, irregular military operations.
- Demonstrate specialised knowledge and integrated understanding of the ethics of armed conflict relevant to special, complex and irregular military operations.

Subject: Space Cooperation, Conflict and Competition (ZHSS8504)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: More satellites have been launched in the last 15 years, than in the preceding 50, starting from the launch of Sputnik I in 1957. If the plans of just 10% of satellite operators proposing new satellites and constellations of satellites were to be realised, as many as 100,000 satellites will be launched in the next 10 years. What accounts for this exponential growth? Can and should it be sustained? The exploration and use of the space domain was born out of great power competition and under the spectre of conflict between the USSR and the US. Yet, over the early decades after the dawn of the space age, exploration of outer space was often characterised by cooperation. In the last decade, an increasing number of States, non-State and commercial actors have sought to exploit the commercial, strategic and scientific opportunities of this domain. Sometimes they cooperate, there is always competition, and the spectre of conflict in space itself looms larger than it ever has. Under-regulated conflict and competition could make Earth orbits and beyond unusable in the near future and for generations to come, with potentially devastating consequences. The course invites you to undertake a learning journey in astropolitics and to set your own personal learning goals. As a core course in the Master of Space Operations program it gives students a detailed understanding of the economic, strategic and political drivers of space activities. It facilitates the intersection of multiple disciplines – particularly in the humanities (history, public policy, economics, commerce, strategy, law, ethics and politics) – with the technical foundations of space operations. Students will gain insights into how these drivers influence planning, policies, acquisitions and operations in respect to space activities. As an elective in other Master programs, the course provides advanced knowledge of the factors shaping the strategic and security environment and policy challenges in the space domain. Students will gain insights into the emerging potential for conflict and about the impact of disruptive space technologies in both the space and terrestrial domains. Students will also gain an appreciation of how the space domain may support conventional and special operations and may be an arena for irregular warfare involving non-state actors, especially commercial entities supporting military activities.
Learning Outcomes:
- Demonstrate an applied understanding of the basic elements of select disciplines (science, history, commerce, strategy, law, ethics and policy) in relation to the space domain.
- Synthesise knowledge across select disciplines to argue a position about cooperation, competition and/or conflict in the space domain (astropolitical situation).
- Formulate an original idea about an astropolitical situation and demonstrate high level communication skills to advocate for the idea.
- Demonstrate enquiry-based learning through critical reflection on, and integration of, new disciplinary and/or professional knowledge of astropolitical situations.
