Subject: Biodiversity & Systematics (BIOL3209)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: This course examines the processes that drive the diversity and distributions of plants and animals. It will cover topics about the basic units of biodiversity - species -and how new species are discovered, recognised and described. We cover the phylogenetic basis of classification systems, and the different methods used to reconstruct evolutionary history and to test the origin of traits, including those relevant for ecology and adaption. We discuss processes that are thought to contribute to lineage diversity, and how biodiversity has changed through time and over large geographic scales. Case studies come primarily from plants and animals (both terrestrial and marine), but with some reference to other organisms. Examples will largely be drawn from, but not limited to, Australian examples.
Learning Outcomes:
- Use tools in the Spatial Portal of Atlas of Living Australia to address questions about biodiversity
- Obtain observation data and map species occurrences (in R)
- Construct maps of species richness (in R)
- estimate a phylogeny using DNA sequence data
- use DNA-barcoding to identify an unknown organism
- implement species distribution modelling (environmental niche modelling) using maxent
- interpret population genetic data
- explain the meaning of "systematics" and why it is important in understanding biodiversity
- demonstrate an understanding of biodiversity and diversification, and how these are reflected in present-day environments.
- explain how organisms are classified and how the different schools of classification differ
- critically evaluate inferences of phylogenetic relationships
- interpret a phylogeny in a biological context
- critically evaluate a taxonomic paper
- elucidate the importance of biodiversity to modern human society
- present the results of biodiversity and evolutionary studies orally
- explain the importance of biodiversity collections (museums and herbaria
- demonstrate an understanding of how organisms are named and the importance of type specimens
Assessment Tasks:
- Weekly in-class quizzes for each learning module Hurdle Identity Verified In-person: 50%% (Hurdle: True)
- Project applying practical skills: 50%% (Hurdle: False)
Workload: Lecture 2 Hours/ Week Practical 3 Hours/ Week 2L3P

Subject: Vertebrate Diversity & Evolution (BIOL3320)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: This course will cover of all aspects of vertebrate evolution and diversity through space and time, delivered with a focus on Australian species. It will explore the main living and extinct vertebrate groups (fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals) with Australian examples, based on lectures and matching hands-on pracs. Subsequently, lectures and hands-on pracs will examine the basic body plan, diversity/ taxonomy/ ID, evolution, phylobiogeography, biomechanics (feeding and locomotion), and current topics of interest (such as conservation or biomedical relevance).
Learning Outcomes:
- Discuss Australian vertebrate morphology and diversity in an evolutionary context
- Identify and taxonomically place a range of vertebrate animals
- Exhibit competency in dissection skills
- Present morphological and anatomical observations in written and drawn form
- Understand the unique features of the Australian vertebrate fauna
Assessment Tasks:
- Evolution and diversity poster: 30%% (Hurdle: False)
- End of Semester Practical Exam Hurdle Identity Verified In-person: 30%% (Hurdle: True)
- End of Semester Examination Hurdle Identity Verified In-person: 40%% (Hurdle: True)
Workload: Lecture 3 Hours/ Week Practical 3 Hours/ Week

Subject: Risk Management (OHSS3002)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: This course has been designed to consolidate students knowledge of OHS risk management principles and practices and introduce them to the broader application of risk management across all aspects of organisational risk. Students will obtain a comprehensive understanding of the risk management process as outlined in AS/NZS ISO 31000. Additionally they will develop advanced skills in the application of the various tools and techniques that may be used to communicate, consult, establish the context, identify, analyse, evaluate and treat organisational risks.
Learning Outcomes:
- Demonstrate an understanding of the development of risk management as a tool for both strategic management and occupational health and safety.
- Use a range of the available methods for hazard identification and risk assessment in the workplace.
- Demonstrate an understanding of the strengths and limitations of various risk assessment tools.
- Select and apply various industry systems and standards for risk management.
- Identify and demonstrate an understanding of the various elements which constitute a risk management plan for an organisation.
- Effectively communicate the outcomes of the risk management process.
Assessment Tasks:
- In-Class Exam #1 Identity Verified In-person: 25%% (Hurdle: False)
- In-Class Exam #2 Identity Verified In-person: 35%% (Hurdle: False)
- End of Semester Exam Hurdle Identity Verified In-person: 40%% (Hurdle: True)
Workload: Workshop 42 Hours/ Ad Hoc 3 x intensive weekend sessions

Subject: Science Engagement in the Community (SCIE3010)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: Science communicators introduce complex scientific information to diverse audiences. This is a project-based course where you will develop, deliver, and evaluate your own science engagement strategy or activity with a community audience of your choosing, underpinned by key principles and frameworks for impactful and influential science communication. You will maintain a detailed project portfolio that you could adapt into an accompaniment to your future CV. SCIE3010 is a compulsory course in the Science Communication minor, but a recommended elective course for any student interested in science communication and education.
Learning Outcomes:
- Characterise audiences based on their demographics, priorities, values, and communication preferences.
- Develop and refine genre and purpose of engagement strategy based on your audience.
- Develop and deliver a meaningful engagement strategy or activity that facilitates audience participation with science.
- Evaluate the impact of an engagement strategy or activity.
- Demonstrate proficiency in stakeholder engagement specific to science communication.

Subject: Data Science for Life and Environmental Scientists (SCIE3360)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: Large data sets are becoming increasingly common in both the life and environmental sciences. They are also vital for addressing global challenges such as biodiversity loss, climate change and global pandemics. Consider for example satellite data capturing global coral reef biodiversity, worldwide daily temperatures measured over decades, or genome sequences of thousands of viruses. This course will provide you with the tools to handle, visualise and analyse such large data sets. Topics covered include data processing (cleaning, exploring & wrangling), an overview of key data types and structures (e.g., spatial, textual, and genomic data), key concepts of data visualisation, an introduction to machine learning, and a primer in simulations. A major focus of this course will be on reproducible research and communication. Throughout the course we will use the programming language R. No prior knowledge of programming or advanced statistics (beyond STAT1201) is assumed.
Learning Outcomes:
- Import, explore, analyse and visualise complex data sets.
- Create visualisations that display complex data in creative ways.
- Apply principles of reproducible data science.
- Understand basic concepts of machine learning and implement machine learning models in R.
Assessment Tasks:
- Quizzes Online: 10%% (Hurdle: False)
- Project 1 Hurdle Identity Verified In-person: 40%% (Hurdle: True)
- Practical Portfolio: 10% Pass/Fail% (Hurdle: False)
- Project 2: 40%% (Hurdle: False)
Workload: Workshop 2 Hours/ Week Practical 3 Hours/ Week

Subject: Research Methods (SCIE4000)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: This course equips students with fundamental and transferable skills in conducting research both within and outside of their disciplinary areas through a series of workshops designed to build on their scientific research knowledge. Opportunities will be provided for students to gain first-hand experience of research activities outside of their own disciplinary area in the Faculty of Science through field trips and site visits, and students will be required to work in multidisciplinary groups for workshop activities. This course is designed to complement and enhance students' experience of their own research project. Students should complete this course in their first semester of Honours year.
Learning Outcomes:
- Develop a research-focussed approach to the solution of a new problem, beginning with the development of research questions.
- Apply project management techniques to plan and carry out a research project in the allocated time frame, within the available resources and in a safe and ethical manner.
- Competently search the scientific literature to critically evaluate previous research.
- Offer clear and logical explanations of scientific concepts that may be unfamiliar to the non-specialist through both oral and written communication.
- Incorporate Indigenous perspectives in research in a respectful and meaningful way.
- Apply fundamental data analysis tools to analyse quantitative and qualitative results.
- Be familiar with the contexts of scientific research, and the various research activities that take place within the Faculty’s schools and research centres.

Subject: Honours Research Project (SCIE4041)
Credit Points: 12
Overview: This two-semester research project course forms the central activity of your Honours program. You will complete a research project under the supervision of academic staff. Activities will include but are not limited to: project design and management, literature review, data collection, data analysis and evaluation, and communication of results. This course provides the training necessary for future studies such as a PhD, and develops advanced knowledge and skills that will give you an advantage when applying for professional work in your disciplinary area. - Contribute to the advancement of relevant scientific fields through original research
- Critically evaluate existing research and identify gaps in knowledge relevant to your project
- Conduct an independent research project including: documenting project progress, generating appropriate evidence through a reproducible method, objectively analysing data, developing evidence-based arguments to support conclusions, and critically evaluating the impact and limitations your research
- Explain your ideas effectively to a general audience by using effective audio-visual means
- Report the rationale and findings in a written document
