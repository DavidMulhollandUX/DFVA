This program is the first of its kind in Australia, offering a unique mix of fundamental and applied physics to prepare you for a career in the growing field of quantum technology. It builds on the outstanding quantum science for which UQ is internationally renowned. Building on your existing technical qualifications in engineering, computer science or mathematics, you will develop a foundation in the principles of quantum physics. You will study advanced topics in quantum physics and quantum information, and gain research-level experience in leading experimental labs. You will graduate with a combination of high-level technical skills, a capacity for creative problem solving in a high-tech environment, and the ability to apply quantum physics theory to research projects in a laboratory setting. As technology companies worldwide race to join the emerging second quantum revolution, you will graduate as a highly skilled and sought-after specialist equipped to collaborate on solving key industry problems in communications, simulators, sensors and computing.

Subject: Photonics (COMS4113)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: Introduction to modern optics and photonics for applications such as telecommunications, sensing and imaging. Topics cover both the wave and particle nature of light, including diffraction, coherence, waveguides, polarisation and resonators. Topics also include semiconductor light sources such as LEDs and VCSELs, as well as photodetectors.
Learning Outcomes:
- Analyse and explain the principles of operation of photonic devices
- Model the photonic devices using analytical and numerical methods and reflect on the results
- Evaluate how the performance of the photonic device affects the behaviour of an optoelectronic system.
- Analyse subsystems that contain photonic devices by means of analytical and simulation techniques.
Assessment Tasks:
- Take Home Assignment: 15%% (Hurdle: False)
- Simulation Assignment: 16%% (Hurdle: False)
- Laboratory Reports: 24%% (Hurdle: False)
- Final exam Hurdle Identity Verified In-person: 45%% (Hurdle: True)
Workload: Lecture 2 Hours/ Week Tutorial 2 Hours/ Week Practical 3 Hours/ Ad Hoc Practical occurs 3 time during the semester

Subject: Topics in Mathematical Physics (MATH4108)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: Mathematical structures and symmetries of physical systems. Illustrative examples may be drawn from classical and quantum many-body systems, integrable and (quasi-)exactly solvable models, conformal field theory, and supersymmetry.
Learning Outcomes:
- Discuss and interpret the importance of symmetry in the development of theories in mathematical physics.
- Explain how algebraic structures provide the mathematical framework to describe symmetries
- Understand the continuum limit of lattice models that leads to (quantum and statistical) field theories.
- Employ renormalisation techniques in field theories and understand universal features of critical phenomena.
- Apply and evaluate appropriate methods for the analysis of various classical and quantum systems in mathematical physics.
Assessment Tasks:
- Assignments: 30% 5% for assignments 1 and 3; 10% for assignments 2 and 4% (Hurdle: False)
- In-Semester Exam Identity Verified In-person: 35%% (Hurdle: False)
- End of Semester Exam Identity Verified In-person: 35%% (Hurdle: False)
Workload: Lecture 3 Hours/ Week Applied Class 1 Hour/ Week

===== SOURCE: https://my.uq.edu.au/programs-courses/course.html?course_code=PHYS4030 =====
Subject: Condensed Matter Physics (PHYS4030)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: It will be shown how the electronic properties of crystals can be understood in terms of quantum mechanics and statistical mechanics. What distinguishes metals, semiconductors, and insulators? The course will be useful to students in physics, chemistry, and materials and electrical engineering.
Learning Outcomes:
- Develop an understanding of the physical quantities relevant to the electronic properties of crystals.
- Develop an understanding of how quantum mechanics and statistical mechanics can be used to understand the electronic properties of crystals.
- Understand the respective assumptions, successes and failures of the Drude, Sommerfeld and Bloch models for the electronic properties of crystals.
- Understand how Blochs theorem leads to energy band structures for crystals.
- Understand how the crystal structure of a material can determine whether it is a metal or insulator.
- Understand how the band structure of a material determines its electronic and optical properties.
- Understand the basics of amorphous solids
- Solve simple quantitative problems and produce answers with the correct physical units.
- Develop an understanding of magnetism and superconductivity as collective many-body phenomena due to the interactions between electrons.
- Have a feel for the orders of magnitude of relevant physical quantities.
- Appreciate that condensed matter physics is a diverse and exciting field with many outstanding problems.
- Be able to relate theory and experiment.
Assessment Tasks:
- Assignments: 30% Weighted equally% (Hurdle: False)
- Presentation Identity Verified: 10%% (Hurdle: False)
- End of Semester Exam Hurdle Identity Verified In-person: 60%% (Hurdle: True)
Workload: Lecture 3 Hours/ Week Applied Class 1 Hour/ Week 3L 1A

Subject: Quantum Field Theory (PHYS4040)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: This course covers advanced topics from single-particle quantum mechanics, such as advanced quantum dynamics, path-integrals, and quantum decoherence. It will also cover introductory non-relativistic many-body quantum theory, relativistic quantum mechanics, and relativistic quantum field theory. We will look at selected applications from atom optics, condensed matter physics, and particle physics.
Learning Outcomes:
- Use the second-quantised formalism of quantum mechanics to describe systems of identical particles, including both bosons and fermions.
- Use symmetry considerations to derive conserved quantities.
- Quantise a field theory and determine the properties of the resulting particles, including their charge, spin, and statistics.
- Use the techniques of relativistic quantum mechanics to calculate amplitudes in scalar field theory and in quantum electrodynamics.
- Describe the structure of the Standard Model of particle physics.

Subject: Atomic Physics & Quantum Optics (PHYS4055)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: This course provides an introduction to the physics of lasers, atom-light interactions, and the quantization of the electromagnetic field. The implications for the conceptual foundations of quantum mechanics and nonclassical effects which arise will be considered.
Learning Outcomes:
- Understand the quantum formalism used to describe the properties of atoms and molecules.
- Calculate features of atomic and molecular systems such as emission spectra, energy level structure and allowed transitions.
- Describe and apply both semiclassical and quantum theories of atom-light interaction, recognising their respective domains of applicability.
- Recognise and quantitatively describe the kinds of optical phenomena that classical theories of light cannot predict.
- Understand how the electromagnetic field can be quantized, and be able to describe mathematically some important quantum states of light.
- Use the formalism of quantum optics to solve problems relating to the manipulation of the quantum states of light.
- Describe the typical kinds of optical elements used in quantum optics experiments and their role in manipulating the state of light.
- Understand the application of atomic and molecular physics as well as quantum optics to some important current research endeavours, such as high precision metrology, quantum information, and tests of fundamental physics.
Assessment Tasks:

Subject: Computational Physics II (PHYS4070)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: This course focuses on the design and implementation of effective, maintainable software projects for scientific computing, using a compiled programming language. Students will learn and apply modern practices, including optimisation, parallelisation, unit testing, documentation, and debugging. These techniques are applied to a range of challenging classical and quantum problems, including, Monte Carlo methods, gravitational-wave signal detection, Hartree¿Fock modelling of many-electron atoms, and selected many-body quantum systems. - Understand the theory of solving equations of motion for physical systems such as orbital mechanics, waveguides, resonances and quantum systems.
- Able to formulate a problem in a suitable form for numerical solution, and the able to produce a computer program to obtain the solution and verify the correctness of the result.
- Apply knowledge of stochastic methods, random numbers, and Monte Carlo techniques to the solution of a wide variety of integral and differential equations
- Describe and analyse the results of a numerical calculations in the form of a scientific report.

Subject: Dynamics & Relativity (PHYS7200)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: Introduction to Lagrangian & Hamiltonian mechanics including elementary treatment of chaos in Hamiltonian systems. Calculus of variations, constraints, generalised coordinates, geometrical methods. Introduction to Special Relativity including relativistic particle mechanics.
Learning Outcomes:
- Solve elementary mechanical problems using the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formalisms.
- Solve basic problems in the calculus of variations.
- Draw phase-space portraits of elementary dynamical systems and identify the various features.
- Understand what is meant by "chaotic dynamics" in terms of sensitive dependence on initial conditions.
- Understand how chaos arises in non-linear Hamiltonian systems, and be able to determine which initial conditions lead to chaos for driven systems with one degree of freedom.
- Understand the principles of special relativity.
- Understand 4-vectors and use them to solve simple relativistic mechanical problems.
Assessment Tasks:
- Problem sheets: 40% : 2 x 6% each for special relativity + 2 X 6% each for chaos + 2 X 8% each for dynamics% (Hurdle: False)
- End of Semester exam Hurdle Identity Verified In-person: 60% 24% dynamics, 18% chaos, 18% special relativity% (Hurdle: True)
