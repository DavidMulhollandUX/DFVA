## Description
The Master of Economics is a specialist program providing you with advanced training in theoretical and applied aspects of modern economics and econometrics.

The Master of Economics provides you with in-depth knowledge of current thought in microeconomics, macroeconomics and econometrics.

## Admission Requirements
**Minimum Entry Requirements**:
Applicants must have one of the following qualifications:

- A qualification equivalent to an Australian Honours degree in Economics or a cognate discipline* with a minimum WAM of 70; or

- A qualification equivalent to an Australian double Bachelor degree where one of the majors/degrees is in Economics and the other is in one of the cognate disciplines* with an overall program WAM of at least 70; or

- A qualification equivalent to an Australian Bachelor degree where either major or degree is in Economics or a cognate discipline* with an overall WAM of at least 70 plus a minimum of two years relevant professional experience.

*Cognate disciplines include Econometrics, Finance, Mathematics, Statistics, Data Science, and Engineering.

Subject: Policy Applications of Behavioural Economics (ECON6312)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: ​Behavioural economics can help explain why real-world decisions sometimes differ from the predictions of mainstream economic theory. This course examines how behavioural insights can be applied thoughtfully to incentives and policy design in a variety of real-world settings. You will first consider gaps in mainstream economic theory and the ways behavioural economics has developed to address them. The course then uses frontier behavioural economics theory and case studies of policy interventions in Australia and abroad to examine the design and consequences of behavioural policies. By the end of the course, you will understand how theoretically grounded behavioural insights can enhance traditional economic analysis and inform the stewardship of modern economies.​
Learning Outcomes:
- Articulate the policy relevance of behavioural economics, including designing policy solutions based on it.
- Develop succinct and clear work that communicate ideas in a logical, professional manner.
- Reflect on the future of behavioural economics and its integration into mainstream applied economics.
- Apply insights from behavioural economics in understanding modern real-world policy problems.
- Recognise the gaps in mainstream economic theory that behavioural economists have sought to fill

Subject: Public Economics and Regulation (ECON6313)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: ​Government intervention affects markets, private behaviour and social welfare. This course investigates the role of the public sector in the economy: where intervention may be needed, why it may be justified, how it shapes private-sector behaviour, and what welfare effects it creates. You will study welfare economics, optimal taxation, market failure, economic regulation, industrial policy and political economy. The course also uses a political economy lens to examine how public-sector behaviour is shaped by political processes as well as economic concerns. Additional video lectures introduce topics such as collusion, financial stability and shadow banks, giving a broader view of government policy. By the end of the course, you will be able to analyse the rationale, design and consequences of government intervention using economic reasoning.​
Learning Outcomes:
- Identify and explain, using verbal, mathematical, and graphical tools, the primary economic reasons for government intervention in a market.
- Evaluate and compare the economic efficiency and likely impacts of government policies on the market.
- Develop succinct and clear work that communicate ideas in a logical, professional manner.
- Compare and contrast different types of taxation mechanisms in the dimensions of equity and efficiency.
- Identify the different groups likely to experience costs and benefits from the introduction of a new regulation or tax/subsidy.

Subject: Microeconomic Theory I (ECON7001)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: Understanding how individuals and firms make optimal decisions and how their interactions shape market outcomes is paramount. It underpins much of modern economics, helping explain and predict behaviour under different market structures and information conditions. This course deals with individual optimal decision making and equilibrium. Fundamental topics covered include rational choice, utility maximisation, expenditure minimisation, production theory, and decision making under uncertainty. Partial equilibrium, game theory and non-competitive environments will also be covered. The course also explores economic environments with asymmetric information, including auctions and screening. By the end of the course, you will be able to formally analyse strategic and non-strategic interactions in economic settings using microeconomic theory.
Learning Outcomes:
- Employ advanced mathematical methods to analyse and assess economic models and their implications.
- Critically evaluate and interpret formal mathematical models of economic phenomena.

Subject: Macroeconomic Theory I (ECON7002)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: ​​This is an advanced course in macroeconomic theory. The first part of the course will briefly review business cycle facts, real business cycle models, and useful tools and technique. The second part of the course will cover the New Keynesian approach to macroeconomics, covering topics such as monetary policy and inflation, the real effects of monetary policy, the advantages of credible policy commitments and monetary-fiscal interactions. By the end of this course, you will be able to analyse macroeconomic dynamics with formal models and evaluate fiscal and monetary policy in a wide range of environments.
Learning Outcomes:
- Recognise and critically assess numerical tools used to solve rational expectations models and analyse their quantitative predictions.
- Synthesise ideas through succinct and clear work that communicates ideas in a logical, professional manner.
- Analyse and explain the assumptions and structure of standard macroeconomic models.
- Critically manipulate these macroeconomic models and assess the implications of their structure.
- Apply macroeconomic models to interpret and analyse problems in macroeconomics.

Subject: Mathematical Economics (ECON7004)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: Mathematical economics is essential because it provides the formal tools to express economic ideas, uncover relations not easily observed through intuition alone, and engage confidently with modern economic research and policy analysis. This course offers a rigorous introduction to mathematical economics. It equips students with the tools to analyse optimisation and equilibrium in diverse economic settings. Building on core concepts such as sets, functions, normed spaces, linear algebra, and calculus, students will apply mathematical techniques including advanced optimisation, fixed point theory, and comparative statics to analyse a range of economic problems. Emphasis is placed on both mathematical formalism and the intuition needed to apply these results in economics. By the end of the course, students will be equipped to engage deeply with analytical economic models and have a rigorous foundation for advanced economic analysis.
Learning Outcomes:
- Formulate and communicate complex economic ideas using mathematical language.
- Employ formal mathematical methods to construct, analyse, and assess economic models and their theoretical implications.
- Critically evaluate and extend advanced mathematical concepts and methods central to economic analysis.

Subject: Macroeconomic Theory II (ECON7102)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: ​Modern macroeconomic analysis often requires solving, parameterising and interpreting formal models. This course introduces techniques for solving and analysing macroeconomic models and applying them to economic policy questions. The course begins with linearisation techniques and perturbation before moving to non-linear approximations of model solutions. You will also learn how to parameterise macroeconomic models, including calibration and basics of estimation such as the Kalman filter and maximum likelihood estimation. The course concludes with an introduction to models with heterogeneous agents. Throughout the course, you will consider how macroeconomic models can be used for economic and policy analysis.​
Learning Outcomes:
- Analyse and explain the assumptions and structure of standard macroeconomic models.
- Select and apply numerical tools to solve rational expectations models and analyse their quantitative predictions.
- Analyse and critically manipulate standard macroeconomic models.
- Apply macroeconomic models to interpret and analyse problems in macroeconomics and construct sophisticated arguments based on these concepts.
- Synthesise ideas through succinct and clear work that communicates ideas in a logical, professional manner.

Subject: Empirical Methods for Causal Inference (ECON7202)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: How can we measure the impact of economic interventions? Why do some policies succeed while others fail? This course provides the powerful tools of causal inference that can answer such questions. It covers foundational methods for identifying causal effects—including randomised controlled trials, difference-in-differences, instrumental variables, and regression discontinuity designs—to equip students with the skills to critically evaluate empirical research and conduct their own rigorous analyses. By the end of the course, you will be able to evaluate and apply causal inference methods to real data to measure the effects of a wide range of policies and programs. Whether you are interested in public policy, labour markets, health, development, or finance, mastering causal inference will enhance their ability to make informed data-driven decisions and produce credible economic analysis.
Learning Outcomes:
- Design and apply advanced techniques to achieve robust causal identification in challenging policy settings.
- Critically evaluate the advanced advantages and limitations of policy evaluation methods (e.g., RCTs, DiD, IV, RDD, Event Studies) across diverse empirical contexts.
- Critically assess and synthesize the empirical implementation of policy evaluation methods, providing nuanced evaluations of their appropriateness and limitations in applied research.
- Identify and explain in depth the distinction between correlation and causation in complex research applications.

Subject: Macroeconometrics (ECON7204)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: ​​This course will provide you with an introduction to econometrics as it is applied in macroeconomics. Emphasis is on hands-on implementation of the methods covered in the course. Topics include macroeconomic data, linear and nonlinear time series models, practical issues with likelihood-based inference for these models, forecast evaluation, structural identification and use of micro data in empirical macroeconomics. By the end of this course, you will be able to implement core macroeconometric methods, evaluate model assumptions, and draw defensible conclusions from aggregate data in order to undertake modern macroeconomic research.​
Learning Outcomes:
- Critically evaluate applied econometric research and use statistical software proficiently to conduct econometric analysis.
- Solve cases associated with identification using macroeconomic data.
- Analyse what constitutes good applied econometric practice.
- Assess and analyse common pitfalls, problems, and solutions that arise in applied econometric work.

Subject: Industrial Organisation (ECON7301)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: ​Industrial organisation studies how firms behave, how markets are structured, and how regulation affects market outcomes. This course gives you tools and knowledge for critically reading recent research papers and conducting original research in industrial organisation. You will investigate how firms exercise market power in different settings and how governments may limit negative effects through regulation. The course develops rigorous theoretical models while maintaining close connections to real-world implications. Topics include market and industry structure, firms' pricing and non-price strategies, productivity, demand, markups, supplier networks, multi-product firms, industrial policy, and contemporary policy debates. By the end of the course, you will be able to use theoretical and empirical perspectives to analyse firms, industries, regulation and industrial policy at a research-oriented level.​
Learning Outcomes:
- Evaluate the welfare implications of strategic behaviour.
- Model the behaviour of monopolists in different circumstances.
- Critically evaluate the approaches to complexities and challenges in real-world data on firm and industry dynamics.
- Solve game theory problems related to the industry structure and the determinants of strategic behaviour in oligopolistic markets.
- Develop succinct and clear work that communicates ideas in a logical, professional manner.

Credit Points: 6
Overview: ​Economic measurement shapes how economists understand data, evaluate policy and interpret economic performance. This course covers the theory and practice of measuring key economic indicators, including the Consumer Price Index, Gross Domestic Product and productivity growth. You will examine approaches used by international statistical agencies and consider how policy implications can depend on measurement techniques. The course is technically rigorous, drawing on microeconomic theory, econometric analysis and recent international research developments. By the end of the course, you will have a stronger foundation for understanding data sources, uses and reliability, and for applying rigorous reasoning to economic measurement problems at an advanced research level.​
