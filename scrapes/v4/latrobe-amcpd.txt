## Master of Community Planning and Development - Bendigo
##### AMCPD
##### 240 credit points
You are viewing the 2026 version

### Overview
The Master of Community Planning and Development will see you working with communities in urban, regional and rural areas, with community development planning agencies and in an international setting. You will also work with non-government organisations and have exposure to a range of rural and regional development issues including: community development, healthy urban design, food security, child friendly and active environments, land-use planning and environmental and social policy at local, regional, national and international levels.

### Course intended learning outcomes
On successful completion you will be able to:
**1.**
Demonstrate ethical decision making and professional planning practice in diverse urban, regional and rural settings
**2.**
Communicate knowledge, skills and ideas clearly with specialist and non-specialist audiences
**3.**
Design innovative solutions to complex social, economic and environmental processes at different scales, for changing urban, regional and rural governance and policy developments
**4.**
Demonstrate an advanced and integrated understanding about creating sustainable towns and cities, and the local and global processes that influence change
**5.**
Apply planning theory, spatial concepts and research techniques to autonomously design and execute a substantial research project

### Course features
### Work based learning (placement) requirements
Compulsory placements (Work Based Learning)
During this course, you will participate in one or more supervised Work Based Learning (WBL) placements designed to allow you to practice newly acquired skills, behaviours and knowledge while integrating theory with practice. The placements will immerse you in professional work culture, enabling you to develop and further enhance your skills. WBL provides you with the opportunity to gain valuable industry experience at one or more of our local, interstate or international industry partners or agencies. WBL placements will generally be arranged by the College based Placements Operation Team or Experiential Learning Team at La Trobe University. The number of hours undertaken can vary considerably, depending on the requirements of the course. Students may incur additional costs and it may be necessary to travel or relocate to complete a specific placement. Please refer to the individual subject entries for more information.
### Career outcomes
Graduates are likely to find diverse opportunities within government departments, including local governments, NGOs, research organisations, advocacy bodies and in the private sector in roles such as community development and management, advocacy, research, public policy development, social and community planning and urban, rural and environmental planning as part of multi-disciplinary teams in social and economic development projects in developed and developing countries.

There is currently a shortage of skilled workers in land use planning, environmental management, community development and public policy development.

Subject: ARCHAEOLOGY OF ABORIGINAL VICTORIA (ARC5AAV)
Credit Points: 15
Overview: In this subject, you will learn about thousands of years of Aboriginal culture and management of Country in southeastern Australia, with particular reference to Victoria. Aboriginal voices and perspectives are foregrounded across a wide range of themes through both recorded and in-person conversations. You will learn about the many different sources of knowledge about culture and Country, including traditional knowledge, archaeological, environmental, and historical evidence. Throughout the subject, you will investigate conflicting explanations for social, technological and demographic change. You will also investigate the role of contemporary Aboriginal communities in shaping archaeological and heritage management practice.
Learning Outcomes:
- Identify theoretical approaches and research questions appropriate to the evidence from different types of Aboriginal archaeological sites.
- Interpret and apply the link between theory and data within Victorian archaeology.
- Understand different sources of archaeological evidence of Aboriginal people in Victoria, different taphonomic and environmental issues relating to its interpretation, and its significance to contemporary Aboriginal peoples.
- Understand culturally competent approaches to archaeological investigations of Aboriginal Victoria.

Subject: METHODS IN GIS: FROM DIGGING TO DATA/DIGGING DATA - PREDICTIVE MODELS, MAPPING AND MORE (ARC5GIM)
Credit Points: 15
Overview: This subject looks at some of the pressing current issues in archaeology: Can we predict where sites are? Can we preserve sites digitally (e.g. in 3D)? We will be looking at these issues and more in this subject. As archaeologists we generate vast quantities of data from a diverse range of sources (e.g. spatial data from excavation; dates from dating samples; analytical measurement data from the analysis of stone tools, pottery etc.). This data comes in a range of scales, for example from continental scale data syntheses, landscape level network analyses, site level artefact distribution, right the way down to micro-scale wear on artefacts at the sub millimetre scale. It is impossible to integrate, plot or analyse this data by hand – GIS offers the only way to compile and analyse data from the multiple spatial and temporal scales that archaeological sites generate – and to present these in graphical format, most commonly manifest as maps. A key practical takeaway skill from this subject will be the ability to process raw archaeological data to create a map that complies with Victorian Heritage legislation (e.g. for submission to ACHRIS). You will also explore analysis and 3D presentation of archaeological data.
Learning Outcomes:
- Design and implement appropriate analyses of spatial data
- Prepare and present maps encapsulating spatial analysis to a professional standard
- Interpret, explain and present the results of complex spatial analysis
- Understand and apply the principles of data collection for spatial analysis
- Use GIS software to address a range of analytical problems in archaeology

Credit Points: 15
Overview: This subject explores the way that planning law and policy regulates the use and development of land. You will consider the concepts of sustainability and social equity and assess whether the policy and legal framework created by planning laws forward these principles. You will also consider the processes of strategic planning, development and environmental assessments and appeals and review of decision-making in a range of different practical, cultural and geographical contexts. - Identify and discuss the common law and legislation relevant to planning in Victoria
- Demonstrate understanding of the processes of planning and decision-making in planning, and review of decision-making

Subject: WATER LAW AND POLICY (LAW3WAT)
Credit Points: 15
Overview: Water resources are of increasing economic significance, whilst water use and management is undergoing increasing environmental scrutiny. Water Law is therefore of increasing relevance in Australian and global environmental sustainability debates. Our program will commence with an analysis of the international framework within which Australian water law is situated, from both an environmental and an international trade perspective. It will consider the federal and state agreements which shape most state legislation, and the federalisation of water resource management in the Murray Darling Basin. It will then consider, from both a policy and a practitioner perspective, the common law and legislation governing water law in Victoria, both in relation to urban, irrigation and environmental water entitlements.
Learning Outcomes:
- Demonstrate capacity to identify and analyse issues relating to Australian water law
- Reconceptualise water related problems according to multiple stakeholder perspectives and identify potential remediation mechanisms.
- Effectively communicate analyses of water-related issues utilising a range of critical perspectives
- Critically assess Australian water law and policy and its capacity to fulfil intergenerational obligations and to forward the public good.
- Demonstrate critical understanding of the international legal and policy environment in which Australian water law has been developed and to critically reflect on the interaction between water law and policy and natural, economic, social and cultural systems.

Learning Outcomes:

Subject: INCLUSION IN COMMUNITIES (SWP5IIC)
Credit Points: 15
Overview: In this subject the focus is on social work practice at community and societal levels. It covers conceptual and theoretical underpinnings of community development including principles, such as active citizenship, and participatory democracy from Western and First Nations worldviews . It has a broad focus in that it covers social, economic, political, cultural, environmental and personal development. The emphasis for creating socially just change is on strategic interventions, incorporating for example, a strengths perspective and community organising, social action, social policy and planning approaches. It will examine community development as a framework for engaging people, communities, and organisations in the change process and promoting community building and social well-being. This subject includes professional learning, practical experiences or portfolio activities which contribute to the completion of the Career Ready AdvantageTM award.
Learning Outcomes:
- Critically reflect on personal values, knowledge and skills and their impact on community work practice.
- Design and evaluate community development approaches and contextually appropriate strategies and techniques and communicate these effectively.
- Conceptualise and analyse the nature of communities using relevant theoretical perspectives including Western and First Nations worldviews.
- Apply expert judgement to identify the key roles and skills necessary to engage in effective community development processes and initiatives in a variety of contexts to maximise participation from multiple perspectives.

Subject: SOCIAL POLICY ANALYSIS AND ADVOCACY (SWP5SPA)
Credit Points: 15
Overview: In this subject, you will critically reflect on your experience of policy in social work practice and build your capacity for policy analysis and advocacy. You will appraise social policy theory, in particular, key social welfare ideologies and theories of justice, rights, needs and capabilities. You will apply this policy knowledge to analyse and evaluate contemporary social policy. Learning Outcomes:
- Evaluate rational and political policy process theories and models.

- Critically analyse and evaluate contemporary social policies against social work values.
- Appraise and justify different policy advocacy theories and strategies to selected policy areas.
- Critically reflect on current social policy with reference to the Australian history of social welfare .
