## Description
The two-year Master of Public Health (Extension)/Master of Infectious Disease Intelligence (MPHext/MIDI) dual extension program is an innovative approach to postgraduate health education. Students gain the advanced disciplinary knowledge and skills required to improve population health outcomes specific to the control of infectious diseases and health emergencies, across a range of contexts and settings.

The MPHext gives you the opportunity to extend your knowledge and skills in public health by completing additional research methodology courses and/or an advanced research project in a public health-related topic.

In an era of emerging and re-emerging infectious disease threats, the public health approach to understanding and controlling the spread of infectious diseases in populations requires a multisectoral approach. You will learn core skills including epidemiology, biostatistics, health promotion, health policy planning and management and those required to address the challenges of the social determinants of health. This is complemented by an advanced understanding of the public health aspects of infectious disease surveillance and intelligence, outbreak investigation and response, infection prevention and mitigation.

The research component provides you with additional, specialised skills in research design and execution. These skills are required to generate evidence to improve population health outcomes, preparing you for roles that involve developing research strategy, funding and evaluating research.

Our MPHext/MIDI fosters judgement-ready practitioners who can contribute to improving population health outcomes specific to the control of infectious diseases across a range of contexts and settings, supported by scholarly evidence-based professional practice in public health.

The MPHext/MIDI program is relevant for professionals working or seeking a research or population health career in government, non-government, military and first responder organisations, primary care, community and health service settings in public and private sectors in Australia and internationally.

Subject: Health Promotion and Social Perspectives of Health (PHCM9012)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: Can public health actions be designed to shape social change and to avoid exacerbating social inequalities and discrimination? Can actions to improve population health at global and country levels reach beyond appeals to individual responsibility and address drivers of health that are not under individual control? In this course you will learn to be attuned to public health actions that over-emphasise individual responsibility for population health outcomes. The course gives you foundations for developing inclusive public health interventions that attend to the social drivers of and reasons for people’s actions. You will learn that promoting population health is not about fixing problematic individuals, but involves shaping our broader social, cultural, political, economic and physical environments so that healthier choices become easier choices. You will adopt the role of a practitioner who, before developing public health action, first asks the right questions with the aim of avoiding exclusion or discrimination. You will be challenged to use tools such as root cause mapping to critically and systematically analyze health problems and identify priority health needs that go beyond individual responsibility. You will learn skills in applying best practice health promotion principles and health promotion strategies that involve intersectoral collaboration, engage and empower people and foster community ownership.
Learning Outcomes:
- Integrate relevant and appropriate Ottawa Charter action areas and health promotion strategies in the planning of health promotion interventions.
- Analyse how social determinants prevailing in a community can enable or challenge communities and individuals to make healthier choices.
- Present convincing arguments for the relevance of health promotion for addressing current global and public health problems.
- Evaluate how fundamental social science concepts enable and constrain public health research and practice.
- Demonstrate best practice health promotion principles in the planning of health promotion interventions across a range of settings.
- Analyse the implicit and explicit concepts of social change used in Public Health.

Subject: Immunisation Policy and Practice (PHCM9050)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: Vaccines are one of the most effective ways to prevent and control infectious diseases. However, even the most effective vaccine is only successful if people have access to vaccines and vaccinate - vaccines don’t save lives, immunisation does. How do we translate vaccine science into effective immunisation policy? How can public health practitioners effectively communicate the benefits of vaccination to the public, particularly in the digital era? How can we ensure equitable access to vaccines within different socioeconomic contexts? What skills do you, as public health professionals, need to support evidence-based immunisation policy decision-making? In PHCM9050 Immunisation Policy and Practice you will develop an understanding of the complex issues that shape immunisation policy and practice applicable to a range of vaccine-preventable diseases and country contexts. Through the course you will critically analyse key and interrelated components of immunisation policy and their practical application to sustaining successful immunisation programs, including vaccine development, clinical guidelines, methods to monitor and evaluate immunisation program delivery, including immunisation coverage, vaccine effectiveness, and adverse events monitoring, the social and behavioural drivers of immunisation, vaccine equity and access, and strategies to engage communities and address vaccine hesitancy. Through expert lectures, Australian and globally relevant cases studies, and your assessments, you will develop skills in applying the vast immunisation-relevant evidence-base to implementing and evaluating immunisation programs.
Learning Outcomes:
- Examine the concepts of vaccine equity and access, social and behavioural factors, and public perceptions about vaccine safety and effectiveness, and appraise their impact on immunisation coverage in populations
- Recognise and interpret the complex issues shaping immunisation policy and practice, including changing disease epidemiology, vaccine coverage, adverse events and risk communication and social and behavioural factors, to a range of vaccine-preventable diseases and settings
- Appraise the capabilities and constraints of surveillance systems used to monitor and evaluate immunisation program outcomes and impact, including immunisation coverage, vaccine effectiveness, and adverse events following immunisation
- Compare and critique the range of strategies used to address barriers to vaccination that result in suboptimal immunisation coverage across diverse communities
- Apply and integrate basic concepts of vaccine-derived immunity and pre-licensure regulatory processes to the development of immunisation policy and vaccine delivery strategies
- Distinguish the suitability of different immunisation strategies to different vaccine preventable diseases and evaluate their potential impact on disease transmission and disease epidemiology across populations and settings

Subject: Regression Methods in Biostatistics (PHCM9517)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: We have access to more data than ever before, but how do we find the signal in the noise? And how much trust should we put in the answers we find? We can answer these types of questions with statistical models. This course will introduce you to a family of statistical models called regression models, powerful tools for answering real-world health questions. For example, we can use clinical data to investigate how body composition correlates with lung function in children with cystic fibrosis. The course will cover some theory, with a strong focus on practical application of the methods using statistical software.
Learning Outcomes:
- Identify an appropriate regression model for your research study
- Critically evaluate statistical analyses and present findings at a standard that is sufficient for submission to scientific journals
- Implement statistical analysis using regression models on complex datasets with different types of variables
- Identify and account for confounding and effect modification in epidemiological studies
- Interpret research findings and draw valid conclusions addressing the research question
- Produce analysis scripts using statistical software to foster reproducible and transparent research

Subject: Politics, Surveillance and Public Health (PHCM9522)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: Power shapes developments in public health. It impacts public health work to: build public trust; engage with public concerns about disease, risk and preparedness; develop new modes of disease surveillance; and connect with non-health agencies and actors with the aim of improving population health. Thus, being able to identify how power operates is a useful skill for public health actors as they develop, implement and evaluate priorities, policies, programs and research. For those interested in addressing health inequities and injustices, the ability to analyse how power functions to open and close avenues for effective action is particularly vital. In this course you will learn to differentiate between: approaches to power that frame power as only ever a problem, a means of constraining people, and; ways of examining how power can both enable and constrain changes in public health action. Learning Outcomes:
- Demonstrate an understanding of the relevance of these different concepts of power for analysing public health practice and research
- Articulate a well-developed position on the strengths and/or limitations of using different analytic tools to examine power relations in public health
- Apply a social scientific analytic tool to analyse how power enables and/or constrains contemporary developments in public health
- Identify fundamental differences between the concepts of power used to explain developments in public health

Subject: Bioterrorism and Health Intelligence (PHCM9789)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: Our systems, thinking, training, legislation and policies have lagged far behind momentous changes in science, and leaving us vulnerable to population-level harm from bioterrorism. Synthetic viruses and genetic engineering of pathogens are a reality, with a rapid acceleration of dual-use research of concern (DURC), which is research intended for good which may also be used to cause harm to humans. The public availability of methods for DURC genetic engineering, coupled with the insider threat, poses an unprecedented risk for global biosecurity. The course will provide a grounding in human health aspects of bioterrorism and response, for first-responders, analysts or policy makers from health, emergency management, law enforcement, military or other relevant backgrounds. An overview of bioterrorism past, present and future scenarios will be covered. Case studies in risk analysis, risk mitigation and response will be studied. These will cover engineered transmissible H5N1 avian influenza; distinguishing natural from unnatural epidemics, surveillance tools, rapid intelligence and analysis methods. International health regulations, governance of DURC, insider threat and ethical frameworks will be examined. Models for cross-sectoral collaboration and communication will also be explored. Preparation of first line responders to biohazards will be covered, including personal protective equipment, decontamination, epidemic control measures, post-exposure prophylaxis and vaccines for biosecurity. The course draws on recognised experts with a breath of experience, and has an emphasis on practical learning experiences using real case scenarios. The course can be taken by health professionals and also professionals from defence, security, law enforcement or emergency management. For the latter, who have no health training, additional materials will be provided to introduce you to key concepts prior to commencing the course.
Learning Outcomes:
- Identify natural and aberrant epidemic patterns using principles of epidemiology and determine appropriate mechanisms for flagging and responding to aberrant epidemic patterns from using an assessment of the available surveillance systems.
- Apply principles of risk analysis to evaluate the insider threat as it applies to biosecurity and consider mitigation strategies.
- Critically evaluate dual-use-research of concern in the biological sciences and the related ethical, legal, governance and public health issues.
- Critically evaluate the history, motivations, health aspects and impacts of bioterrorism and the associated risk to human health.
- Apply the principles of occupational safety, prevention, risk mitigation, and management of biothreats to policy and planning for protection of first line responders.
- Map and analyse the components of emergency response to biosecurity threats and epidemic control, and how each component relates to the others.
- Gain diverse perspectives in biosecurity from different stakeholders and disciplines, the gaps and weakness in inter-sectoral collaboration, and models for improving the same.
