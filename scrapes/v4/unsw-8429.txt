## Description
The flexible two-year Master of Applied Economics has been developed to prepare professional economists. Prior learning and/or work experience may be recognised to reduce duration of the program to one year. Graduates will gain broad and advanced knowledge and practical skills in Economics, while developing research and communication skills through coursework and the Economics practicum.

Flexibility in program delivery and learning style is catered for in this program, with multimodal or online study options being available in many core and elective courses.

## Admission Requirements
**Minimum Entry Requirements**:
A bachelor degree from an Australian university or equivalent overseas institution with a minimum overall average of 65%, as determined by the UNSW Postgraduate Coursework Entry Calculator

Students' prior learning will be recognised to reduce the program duration. For students with Bachelor of Economics or Bachelor of Business degree with economics specialisation or equivalent Honours or Graduate Diploma, the 5 core courses may be waived or credited.

For students with prior work experience of at least one year in the private or public sector (for example, clerical or administrative work in a commercial, not-for-profit or public service organisation, supervisory or management, banking, finance or marketing experience or other business-related positions) the prescribed economics practicum may be waived or credited.

NOTE: Assumed knowledge - A level of knowledge equivalent to a first-year undergraduate basic mathematics subject is assumed. Students without such knowledge are advised to contact the Postgraduate Coursework Director in Economics, complete an appropriate self-assessment of university mathematics knowledge before the commencement of their first term and complete a Maths bridging course where appropriate.

Subject: Microeconomics (ECON5101)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: Decision-making by consumers, producers, and governments lies at the heart of economic analysis. It determines how scarce resources are allocated, how markets function, and how welfare is distributed across society. The first half of this microeconomics course is devoted to the study of decision-making and the modelling of associated market behaviour. We then study how individual economic decisions interact in markets with varying degrees of competition, paying substantial attention to the comparative welfare outcomes produced by different market forms. Additional topics include externalities, game theory and its applications, and asymmetric information. There is an emphasis on the use of analytical tools and mathematical models, which underpin much of current research in microeconomics. By the end of the course, students will be able to apply core microeconomic models to analyse decision-making and market outcomes, critically evaluate efficiency and welfare implications across market structures, and use formal tools to address issues such as externalities, strategic interaction, and information asymmetries.
Learning Outcomes:
- Develop and present arguments to demonstrate an understanding of key concepts of microeconomics and their appropriate usage.
- Develop succinct and clear work that communicates ideas in a logical, professional manner.
- Analyse choice problems and markets by identifying and applying appropriate mathematical techniques to design and solve economic models.
- Evaluate environmental and sustainability problems arising from non-cooperative decision making among agents/ firms/countries.

Subject: Economics of Finance (ECON5106)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: The valuation of financial assets is a cornerstone of real-world financial markets. The economic theory of financial markets provides a logical foundation for modern asset pricing and portfolio choice theories. This course provides economic insights into finance and financial markets. We study equilibrium valuation and pricing through the lenses of economic concepts. Major financial instruments such as bonds, stocks, futures, and options are introduced under economic equilibrium context, and we discuss techniques to value these diverse financial assets. We then turn to the "utility-based" approach, wherein a well-defined utility structure is established as a model primitive. Maximising expected utility, we derive optimal consumption, equilibrium trades and prices. In turn, we discuss how trade is beneficial for all the participants in the market. We conclude the course with an introduction to modern portfolio theory.
Learning Outcomes:
- Evaluate and critically analyse the principles underlying the pricing of financial assets whose payoffs are contingent on certain outcomes.
- Evaluate and critically analyse standard asset pricing models, including their underlying assumptions, limitations, and usefulness in financial decision making.
- Employ programming tools and apply the principles of asset pricing to evaluate derivative instruments, assess the factors relevant for valuing contingent-payoff assets, and demonstrate the appropriate application of portfolio formation principles.
- Synthesize ideas through succinct and clear work that communicates ideas in a logical, professional manner.
- Work collaboratively to complete tasks.

Subject: Econometrics (ECON5205)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: What can we learn from data, and how can we separate correlation and causality? This course introduces the use of econometrics to explore and estimate economic relationships using linear regression models. Emphasis will be placed on cross-sectional data and on understanding the conditions required for inferring causal relationships. We will also consider extensions that address common data analysis complications, including heteroskedasticity, proxy variables, and other data limitations. Practical computer-based exercises are integrated throughout, enabling students to gain hands-on experience working with real-world data and econometric software. By the end of the course, you will have the skills to critically interpret empirical relationships and a foundation for more advanced courses in econometrics.

Overview: What determines who gains and who loses in global trade and how governments should respond? International trade shapes economic growth, wages, inequality, and the success or failure of businesses. This course develops an advanced, analytically rigorous analysis of trade theory and trade policy, supported by empirical evidence. Subject: Behavioural Economics (ECON5324)
Credit Points: 6
Overview: ​​Standard economic theory assumes that people have correct beliefs based on correct statistical reasoning, that they are narrowly self-interested, and that they manage to maximise their utility. Decades of experimental evidence suggest a more complicated picture. People systematically misperceive probabilities, cooperate with strangers in ways that sacrifice their own material payoffs, and make choices that they themselves deem suboptimal. Explaining these empirical regularities requires new theory. ​In this course, students will study the core findings of behavioural economics, learn the formal theoretical frameworks developed to explain it, and build the empirical reasoning skills needed to critically evaluate competing accounts. Topics include reference-dependent preferences and prospect theory, intertemporal choice, risk preferences, bounded rationality, social preferences, behavioural game theory, and behavioural public policy. ​By the end of this course, you will be able to formulate behavioural hypotheses precisely, evaluate them using appropriate theoretical tools, and critically assess the evidence used to support or challenge them.​
Learning Outcomes:
- Critically evaluate claims made by behavioural economists and critically apply the tools learned in class to explain real-world phenomena.

- Analyse and interpret findings from behavioural economics research and evaluate their applications to public policy.
