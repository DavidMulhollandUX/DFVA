Subject: Theory of Computing (COMP2048)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: This course provides a rigorous introduction to the Theory of Computation, exploring the fundamental limits of what can be computed and how efficiently computational problems can be solved. Students study formal models of computation through the framework of languages and automata, including deterministic and nondeterministic finite automata (DFA and NFA), pushdown automata (PDA), and Turing machines. The course emphasizes precise mathematical reasoning and proof techniques, and examines fundamental questions of decidability and undecidability. Additionally, the course covers an introduction to Computational Complexity Theory as the study of the resources required to solve computational problems. The course delves into central complexity classes, including P and NP, and explores the practical limits of efficient computation. The course also introduces alternative models of computation, including an introduction to quantum computing, to provide a broader perspective beyond the classical model.
Learning Outcomes:
- Define core concepts in the theory of computation, including computation, algorithms, decision problems, and formal languages.
- Construct formal models of computation using finite automata, pushdown automata, and Turing machines.
- Analyze the expressive power and limitations of different computational models.
- Apply formal proof techniques to establish correctness, equivalence, and impossibility results.
- Determine the decidability or undecidability of computational problems using appropriate theoretical tools.
- Explain computational complexity in terms of resource usage such as time and space.
- Classify computational problems within basic complexity classes, including P and NP.
- Describe the principles of quantum computation and explain how quantum models differ from classical models of computation.
Assessment Tasks:
- Assignment 1 Hurdle: 20%% (Hurdle: True)
- Assignment 2 Hurdle: 20%% (Hurdle: True)
- Assignment 3 Hurdle: 20%% (Hurdle: True)
- Final Examination Hurdle Identity Verified In-person: 40%% (Hurdle: True)
Workload: Lecture 2 Hours/ Week Tutorial 2 Hours/ Week Practical 2 Hours/ Week

===== SOURCE: https://my.uq.edu.au/programs-courses/course.html?course_code=COMP2200 =====
Subject: Ethical Practice in Computing (COMP2200)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: This course introduces students to the ethical dimensions of computing practice, the responsibilities of computing professionals and the critical role of responsible decision-making. Through a case study centred approach, students will explore how everyday design and development decisions can lead to profound social, legal and ethical consequences. Topics include professional codes of conduct, frameworks for ethical decision-making, bias and inclusion, cybersecurity and privacy, sustainability and the ethical challenges of emerging technologies. Students will gain the tools to anticipate the consequences of technological choices beyond mere legal compliance, understand relevant Australian laws and global standards. Students will develop the moral sensitivity and principled reasoning needed for responsible computing.
Learning Outcomes:
- Describe the foundation principles and regulatory frameworks that guide and govern professional practice in computing, including relevant codes of conduct and Australian laws pertaining to technology.
- Apply ethical decision-making frameworks to anticipate the social, environmental and ethical impact of technological products and development processes.
- Explain legal & ethical responsibilities associated with issues related to privacy, security, diversity, inclusion and systems reliability in computing practice.
- Identify and critically reflect on personal biases and their potential influence on technological design, development and deployment.
Assessment Tasks:
- Weekly Tasks: x 2% (Hurdle: False)
- Case Study Hurdle Identity Verified: x 5% (Hurdle: True)
- Final Examination Hurdle Identity Verified: x 3% (Hurdle: True)
Workload: Seminar 3 Hours/ Week Applied Class 2 Hours/ Week

Subject: Operating Systems Architecture (COMP3301)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: Implementation and design techniques for operating systems. Core material includes advanced kernel-level and device driver programming techniques, how operating systems principles are realised in practice, principles and practice of operating system support for distributed and real-time computing, case studies and different approaches to operating system design and implementation, including different models of software ownership.
Learning Outcomes:
- Identify and define key components of operating systems such as the kernel, process management, memory management, and file systems.
- Explain the role and functionality of operating systems in managing hardware and software resources and how software interacts with hardware.
- Create and apply design decisions for low-level operating systems components at the kernel and device driver level.
- Analyse the performance and trade-offs of operating system components like schedulers or file systems.
- Evaluate security features and vulnerabilities of operating systems and propose improvements or alternatives.
- Design a simple operating system module or enhance an existing module, such as a scheduler or a file system, using appropriate programming tools and environments.
Assessment Tasks:

- Programming Assignment Two Hurdle In-person: 25%% (Hurdle: True)
