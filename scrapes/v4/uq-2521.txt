Subject: Introduction to Criminology (CRIM1000)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: What drives criminal behaviour? This course introduces you to criminology's core questions: how we define, measure, and explain crime. You'll examine major theoretical perspectives, learn research methods, and develop the interpretive lenses needed to understand crime causation and criminal justice responses.
Learning Outcomes:
- Explain foundational concepts and theories in criminology: Explain key criminological concepts, definitions, and major theoretical perspectives used to understand criminal behaviour.
- Construct evidence-based criminological arguments: Develop clear, structured, and academically informed arguments supported by relevant research and data.
- Find and synthesise criminological information: Locate, evaluate, and integrate information from diverse sources, including academic research, crime data, and media to inform criminological analysis.
- Apply ethical approaches to criminological research and practice: Identify and critically assess ethical issues in criminological research and professional practice, including responsible use of AI and emerging technologies.
- Demonstrate cultural competency in criminology: Recognise and appreciate how social diversity, inequality, and cultural contexts shape criminal behaviour and justice system responses.
Assessment Tasks:
- Crime Fact Checking Investigation Online: 20%% (Hurdle: False)
- Tutorial Exercises Identity Verified In-person: 40%% (Hurdle: False)
- Theory Application Argument Hurdle Online: 40%% (Hurdle: True)
Workload: Tutorial 1 Hour/ Week Lecture 2 Hours/ Week

Subject: Introduction to Criminal Justice (CRIM1019)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: The course provides an introduction to the criminal justice system in Australia. Students will examine the policies and practices of law enforcement, adjudicative and correctional agencies of the criminal justice system. Topics will include the role of the criminal justice system in social control, the aims and current issues facing the police, courts and corrections, and criminal justice reform.
Learning Outcomes:
- describe the practical operation of and major policies that regulate the criminal justice system in Australia
- understand the culture, practices, and ideologies of actors within the criminal justice system, and the consequences for the processing of cases
- critically understand the implications of criminal justice processes for social exclusion
- critically understand the major concepts and theoretical paradigms in analysing the criminal justice system and its processes
- communicate effectively about criminal justice concepts and issues
- understand how to negotiate group dynamics to achieve a common goal
Assessment Tasks:
- Court Visit Reflection In-person: 10%% (Hurdle: False)
- Group Roundtable Hurdle Identity Verified Team or group-based In-person: 40%% (Hurdle: True)
- Court Research Paper Online: 20%% (Hurdle: False)
- End of Semester Exam Identity Verified In-person: 30%% (Hurdle: False)
Workload: Lecture 1.5 Hours/ Week Tutorial 2 Hours/ Fortnight

Subject: Introduction to Professional Practice in Criminology (CRIM1111)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: This course aims to provide the knowledge and skills to prepare students for future employment in professional areas related to criminology and criminal justice. This course will provide foundational knowledge of the core elements of professional practice and challenge students to explore how these elements lead to better outcomes in a range of different criminological and criminal justice contexts. The course will teach through a combination of lectures, group work and presentations by criminology/criminal justice field experts who will discuss the application of target skills within their profession's practice. Students will also learn tools to develop their own professional framework that can demonstrate their skills and knowledge to potential employers.
Learning Outcomes:
- Demonstrate understanding of the core knowledge, skills and behaviours that inform effective practice in criminology and criminal justice contexts.
- Demonstrate an understanding of the political, social, institutional, ethical, cultural, and legal contexts that shape professional practice in criminal justice and criminology.
- Collaborate effectively as a member of a project team by applying core professional practice skills and problem-solving strategies.
- Engage academic and practice literature relevant to this field of study.
- Recognise and articulate your personal professional practice framework to achieve future career goals.
- Communicate effectively through writing.

Subject: The Policy and Practice of Crime Control and Crime Prevention (CRIM2140)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: In recent years the effectiveness of the criminal justice system has come under sustained criticism. This has demanded policy responses underpinned by strategies and tactics informed by sound, scientific evidence and rigorous program evaluation. This course will teach students to recognise, assess, and generate evidence-based crime policies across a range of contexts and criminal justice domains such as police, courts, and corrections. This will include a focus on how government and non-government agencies can develop "upstream" responses; that is policies and programs that aim to prevent crime before they become "downstream" problems requiring responses by the criminal justice system. This requires an evidence-based approach that emphasises problem solving and analysis. Topics will include program design and evaluation and the course will cover various crime prevention approaches such as crime prevention through environmental design, situational crime prevention, social prevention, and developmental crime prevention.
Learning Outcomes:
- Capacity to recognise, assess and generate evidence-based crime policies.
- Ability to scrutinize the integrity and effectiveness of different crime policies.
- Understanding of the complexities in adopting evidence-based crime policies.
- Ability to identify rigorous evaluation methods to inform evidence-based crime policies
- Work collaboratively with others and complete group work tasks
Assessment Tasks:
- Preliminary assessment: complete blackboard form Hurdle Online: N/A% (Hurdle: True)
- Assessment 1a: Research problem pitch In-person: 10%% (Hurdle: False)
- Assessment 1b: Peer reflection In-person: 25%% (Hurdle: False)
- Assessment 2: Research Report Online: 65%% (Hurdle: False)
Workload: Seminar 3 Hours/ Week

Subject: Policing and Society (CRIM2200)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: This course is organised around key emerging issues in the study of policing, such as police legitimacy, police roles and functions, police behaviour, misconduct and accountability, police use of force, gender issues in policing, police-minority relations, measuring police performance and organisational change and the emergence of new forms of police practices (e.g. third-party, community policing and problem oriented policing). It examines factors that shape the development, organisation and operational practice of modern policing such as police legitimacy and public confidence in the police.
Learning Outcomes:
- Understand and assess police roles and functions and styles of policing.
- Understand the pressures and demands placed on the operations of modern police agencies.
- Identify factors that impact on police legitimacy and police effectiveness.
- Critically evaluate how to improve the operations of modern police agencies.
- Critically examine and evaluate current issues in policing.
Assessment Tasks:
- Discussion Boards Online: 30% 15% per discussion board% (Hurdle: False)
- In-class Quiz In-person: 30%% (Hurdle: False)
- Class Discussion and Worksheets Team or group-based In-person: 40%% (Hurdle: False)

Subject: Advanced Criminological Theory (CRIM3039)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: Departmental consent is required to enrol in this course - please contact the School of Social Science (student.socsci@uq.edu.au) for permission to enrol. This course examines major advances in criminological theory over the past century. The course extends knowledge provided in introductory criminology theory and considers current theoretical debates as well as the current research standing of core theories in the field of Criminology. The course provides students with an opportunity to understand the dimensions and complexities of original theoretical statements as well as empirical challenges in testing theories of criminal behaviour.
Learning Outcomes:
- Build on the theoretical understanding of criminology gained in introductory-level courses
- Develop a deeper understanding of the discipline of criminology
- Examine selected concepts and issues that have emerged (or remain) as important ideas or debates in criminology in the 21st century
- Have a stronger understanding of criminological research practice and the ethical considerations specific to the study of crime
- Demonstrate stronger analytic skills in assessing criminological research
- Work collegially in a small team environment
Assessment Tasks:
- Theory Building Worksheets Team or group-based In-person: 40%% (Hurdle: False)
- Theory Application Paper Online: 30%% (Hurdle: False)
- Theory Driven Solutions Showcase Hurdle Team or group-based In-person Online: 30%% (Hurdle: True)

Subject: Advanced Professional Practice in Criminology (CRIM3111)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: This course is designed to equip students with transferable knowledge and practical skills relevant to key areas in criminology and criminal justice, both established and emerging. Through engaging with industry experts and participating in practice-based activities and assessments, students will gain hands-on experience in three core domains: restorative justice principles and practice, cybercrime and security assessment, and financial crime investigation. Students will develop their skills in response to current issues and build confidence in delivering impactful outputs that are valued by industry.

- Understand the professional practice skills which can facilitate effective performance outcomes in the target topic areas.
- Develop planning strategies incorporating learned principles and practices for relevant target topic area.
- Understand the political, social, institutional, ethical, cultural and legal contexts that shape professional practice for each target topic area.
- Problem-solve by working in collaborative teams and applying course concepts
- Communicate effectively through writing.

Overview: Criminal Law in context involves the study of the general principles of criminal responsibility and procedure and examines how specific offences are made, defined, prosecuted and defended. The course examines how the operation of different institutions and actors (public and private police, prosecution, courts, defendants, juries, corrections) shape the boundaries of the criminal law, focusing on the role of discretion and public interest/policy considerations.
Learning Outcomes:
- Identify and describe key principles underpinning the criminal law, particularly the concept of criminal responsibility.
- Understand how the criminal law operates in relation to specific offences, defences, and contexts.
- Analyse and critically evaluate the criminal law within specific situations, contexts, and crime types.
- Apply effective legal research skills, such as analysis of case law, legislation, and secondary sources, to understand the application of the law and to support arguments re: law reform.
- Demonstrate and apply skills required to communicate effectively within a legal and policy context.

Subject: Transnational Crime and International Criminal Justice (LAWS3330)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: Transnational Crime and International Criminal Justice explores the levels and patterns of organised crime and associated crime types and critically examines the international law and national policies to prevent and suppress these phenomena. The course is interactive and allows students to independently research specific crime types, engage in discussion, and present their findings and opinions.
Learning Outcomes:
- Have a general understanding of the patterns and levels of transnational organised crime and terrorism.
- Describe and critique the nature and problems of transnational organised crime and terrorism.
- Identify key instruments and the strengths and weaknesses of the current system of international criminal justice, especially as it relates to the criminalisation of transnational crime and terrorism and enforcement.
- Navigate the complexity of international and transnational legal, policy, and institutional contexts in order to be able to effectively engage with key debates emerging in this area.
- Work together with others to identify the dimensions of a problem arising from a case study of global or transnational crime, to generate solutions to the problem, and to communicate this to an informed audience.
- Apply skills required to engage in effective legal and academic research, and to communicate effectively within a legal and policy context.

Subject: Introduction to Social Research (SOCY2019)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: This course introduces students to the foundations, tools, and techniques of social research. It does this by embedding questions of method into a broad framework of research design and analysis so that research is approached as a holistic enterprise in which theory, method and analysis are interrelated. Rather than simply offering a suite of different qualitative and quantitative research methods for students to trial, this course takes as its starting point the kinds of questions researchers pose about the social world and the various methodologies and methods that inform these questions. It also encourages students to be critical about their research practice and to consider the ways that research has supported the project of settler colonialism in its treatment of Indigenous peoples and communities. Students will be introduced to Indigenous perspectives on research, not only as subjects of research by non-Indigenous researchers, but as skilled researchers with their own ways of knowing, being and doing. The course also exposes students to the affordances of Generative AI in research and equips them with the skills to become ethical and critical users of GenAI across the research process. On completion of this course, students will be skilled in the art of reflexive, ethical and methodologically rigorous social research.
Learning Outcomes:
- Design methodologically coherent research across all stages of the research process
- Demonstrate a growing capacity to undertake research with Indigenous peoples and communities guided by Indigenous values and priorities
- Make ethical choices in the design and conduct of social research
- Apply and transfer social research skills in a variety of contexts and domains
- Demonstrate critical use and appraisal of Gen. AI in the research process

- Assignment 2 Article Review: 30% 1,000 words% (Hurdle: False)
- Assignment 3 Exam Hurdle Identity Verified In-person: 40% approximately 1,000 words% (Hurdle: True)
Workload: Workshop 2 Hours/ Fortnight

Subject: Introducing Quantitative Research (SOCY2339)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: Quantitative research methods are the most commonly used research methods in the social sciences. They are widely used by academic researchers, government agencies, private companies, and community organisations. This course provides an introduction to quantitative methods in the social sciences. It emphasises how social scientists use simple quantitative techniques to investigate research questions coming from social science theory, prior research and applied problems. The course focuses on the link between theory and research in social science, the logic of quantitative empirical analysis, and techniques for describing quantitative data and drawing inferences (generalisations) about larger populations. The course also introduces Jamovi statistical software for simple quantitative analysis.
Learning Outcomes:
- Understand the stages of quantitative research
- Understand how social theories are related to social scientific concepts
- Be able to calculate and interpret basic univariate descriptive statistics
- Be able to construct and interpret two and three-way cross-tabulations
- Understand the logic of classical statistical inference
- Be able to make statistical inferences based on a sample mean/proportion or comparison of means/proportions
- Be able to carry out a chi-squared test of statistical independence for tabular data
- Be able to construct and interpret a correlation coefficient
- Appreciate the logic of bivariate linear regression
- Be able to use Jamovi statistical software to access and manipulate survey data in preparation for quantitative analysis
- Be able to use Jamovi statistical software to carry out simple descriptive and inferential statistical procedures
Assessment Tasks:
- Mid-Semester Exam (central) Identity Verified In-person: 35%% (Hurdle: False)
- Assignment 2 - Applying Inferential Statistics Online: 50%% (Hurdle: False)
- Review Tests Online: 15%% (Hurdle: False)
Workload: Lecture 2 Hours/ Week Tutorial 1 Hour/ Week

Subject: Qualitative Social Research (SOCY3329)
Credit Points: 2
Overview: Learn sought after skills in qualitative social science research through doing. Covering research design and practice, the course introduces the methods, methodology and epistemology of various analytical approaches: thematic analysis, critical discourse analysis, visual analysis, yarning and participatory action research. The course also explores many contemporary theoretical approaches and debates including interpretivism, social constructionism, post-positivism, feminism, Indigenous methodologies and post-colonial approaches.
Learning Outcomes:
- distinguish the assumptions, theories and concepts which have been developed by various approaches to social inquiry, from post-positivist to interpretivist, critical, participatory and Indigenous methodologies;
- identify the specific concepts, methods, data and approaches relevant to the qualitative exploration and analysis of particular issues, problems and social phenomena;
- generate original research questions informed by relevant theories and concepts, and identify the corresponding methods for ethically generating data to answer these questions;
- identify and evaluate the strengths and limitations of research techniques in qualitative inquiry;
- describe and apply sociological knowledge and understanding through oral and written means of expression;
- collaborate effectively as a member of a social research team.

- CDA Proposal & 'Show Your Work' Write Up: 35% 1,500 words% (Hurdle: False)
